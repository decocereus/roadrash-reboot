# Rushline architecture

Agreed October 6, 2026. This replaces the first prototype, a server-driven lane model with sprite riders. That code is kept in commit `2ba657f`.

## Product

An original motorcycle combat racer for desktop browsers, in the spirit of the mid-1990s Road Rash games: fast point-to-point road races, oncoming traffic, fights between riders, and crashes that throw you off the bike. Single-player comes first. The name, tracks, bikes, riders, art, and audio are our own; RUSHLINE is the working title. "Road Rash" is an Electronic Arts trademark and nothing of theirs is used.

The art direction is cel-shaded cyberpunk anime: two flat tones, ink outlines, neon signs, and a palette of yellow, cyan, magenta, and red on near-black. Proportions are realistic, for the world, the bikes, and the riders. The look was accepted on October 13, 2026 as the base to build on. Colours, faces, and outfits get refined after the rest of the game exists.

## Platform

| Concern | Choice |
| --- | --- |
| Infrastructure as code | Alchemy v2, pinned to the same preview build as Faceless |
| Web app | TanStack Start on Cloudflare Workers, shadcn components in `packages/ui` |
| Rendering | Three.js on WebGL 2 |
| Simulation | Plain TypeScript in `packages/sim`, fixed 60 Hz, running in the browser |
| Server runtime | Effect 4.0.0 on Workers, when a server feature exists |
| Multiplayer (later) | One Durable Object per race running the same simulation |
| Tooling | pnpm, TypeScript 7, Oxlint with the anti-slop rules, Oxfmt through Ultracite, Vitest |

There is no API Worker or database yet, because nothing needs one. Leaderboards, accounts, and multiplayer bring them back.

## Riding model

`packages/sim` owns every rule. It has no DOM, Three.js, clock, or unseeded randomness, so the same inputs always produce the same race. State is plain data that `stepRace` advances by exactly 1/60 second.

A rider lives in track space: distance along the road `s`, lateral offset `x` in metres, speed, and lateral speed. The bike is not a rigid body. The model is the smallest one that produces the behaviour we want, and it steers the way the Road Rash games did:

- **The bars take time.** Holding left or right ramps the bike's sideways speed toward full lock at the bike's `steerRate`, about 6 m/s² on the starter bike, so the bike reaches its full sideways speed of 4 to 5 m/s most of a second after the key goes down, a lane change takes a second and a quarter, and the bike keeps moving sideways for up to most of a second after the key lifts, ramping down at the same rate. A short press therefore moves the bike less than a metre and carries on for about half a second. The lean is only a visual tilt that follows the lateral motion. Steering authority fades below 12 m/s, but a bike under throttle steers as if it were doing at least 3 m/s, so a rider held at a standstill can still steer out.
- **The throttle and the brake take a quarter of a second.** `RiderState.throttle` and `RiderState.brake` follow the input at 4 per second, so a tap costs little, the needle sits still for the first fifth of a second of braking, and a standing start gathers itself before it pulls. The brakes are strong: the starter bike stops from its 190 km/h top speed 2.3 s after the key, in under 70 m, and from 200 km/h in about 55 m on the Sesta. Off the throttle the engine braking is mild, 1.5 m/s² on the starter bike at the top of its revs and less as they fall, so a bike that has shut off keeps three quarters of its speed after three seconds and about half after seven: a rider who lifts for a bend is not punished the way the old coast-down punished them.
- **Curves push you out, and leaning scrubs speed.** A bend drifts the bike outward at `curvature · speed² / curvePush`. A rider who does nothing ends up on the grass, and every bend has a speed above which full steer cannot hold it. Leaning costs forward speed: every second the bike loses `leanScrub × lock² × speed`, where `lock` is the share of its sideways speed the bike is using, with the throttle held or not. The square means a gentle sweeper at a quarter lock costs almost nothing and full lock costs the most: on the Wren at 190 km/h that is 10.6 m/s², about 38 km/h a second, more than the brake gives at full lock. That is the bike slowing itself in a bend. A Wren that turns in and holds the throttle through a 140 m bend arrives at 190 km/h and comes out in the 140s, on the road; a 70 m right angle still runs it wide unless the rider lifts or brakes, and on the Wren a lift is enough. The play test of October 15, 2026 found that bikes kept their full speed in bends, so the push ran every corner wide and the player braked for nearly all of them. Before that the cost was a flat 0.3 m/s² per metre per second of sideways speed, about 1.5 m/s² at full lock, and every `curvePush` was 77% of what it is now, fitted to a lineup 20% slower; the push grows with the square of the speed, so the faster lineup had been pushed 44% harder at its top speed. Lock shares the grip with the brakes: at full lock the brakes have 35% of their bite, so the brakes never vanish in a bend but they do weaken.
- **Grass is slow.** Off the road the bike has heavy drag, 45% of its braking, and 80% of its steering authority, and full throttle on grass settles at about 50 mph on the starter bike. Braking and steering fade to the grass values over the first 0.75 m past the edge, so running wide is a slope, not a wall. A hit stuns steering to 65% for a sixth of a second, so a rider punched onto the grass can still fight back to the road.
- **The engine has gears.** `engine.ts` owns the drive. A `BikeSpec` lists the road speed at each gear's redline in `gears`, and `powerPerMass` is the bike's peak pull. Torque over the rev range is a hump that peaks at 72% of the redline and falls to 45% at idle and to 79% at the redline, and thrust is `throttle × powerPerMass × torque(revs) / (redline speed of the gear × the peak of revs × torque)`, so in every gear the bike pulls hardest in the middle of the band and tails off toward the redline. The box shifts itself: up at 96% of the redline, with an 8 tick cut in which the bike pulls nothing and the revs drop into the next gear's band, and down below 58%; in first the clutch holds the revs at half or more while the throttle is open, and over the redline the limiter cuts the thrust. `launchAcceleration` still caps the pull off the line through the tyre load. `RiderState` exposes `gear`, `revs` (0 to 1.05 of the redline), and `shiftTicks`, which the engine note and the dial read. The Halden Wren 400 reads 27, 58, 83, 109, 126, 139, 151, 159, 167, 174, 178, and 181 km/h at 1 to 12 s of throttle from a standstill, reaches 100 km/h at 3.6 s, shifts at 79, 109, 135, 157, and 178 km/h, and tops out at 190 km/h seated and 198 tucked in. Off the throttle from 179 km/h it reads 162, 147, 134, 122, 112, 103, and 95 at 1 to 7 s. The other eight bikes run from the Gutter Pup (145 km/h, 100 km/h in 7.1 s) to the Nera 1000 (283 km/h, 100 km/h in 2.6 s); the table is in `docs/design/game-design.md`. Before October 14, 2026 the Wren was fitted to the original game's measured starter bike (158 km/h, 60 mph in 6.2 s); the user found that slow, and the lineup now reads like modern road bikes.
- **The rider tucks in.** The tuck is a settling-in, not a reflex. `RiderState.tuckTicks` counts the ticks the rider has held the throttle past 0.8 with no brake, less than 0.3 of steering lock, above 120 km/h, on the road, not swinging and not stunned; at 90 ticks (1.5 s) the rider is settled and `RiderState.tuck` eases toward 1 at 1 per second, so the full tuck is in about 2.5 s after the speed is reached. Once settled the rider stays settled through steering and through a lifted throttle: only a brake past 0.5, a strike, a hit, a crash, leaving the road, or dropping below 95 km/h resets the count and drops the tuck at 5 per second. A steering tucked rider stays tucked but rises a little: the target is `1 − 0.25 × lock`, so at full lock the tuck is 0.75 and comes back to 1 at the same 1 per second when the bars straighten. A full tuck cuts drag by 14%, which lifts top speed by about 4% on every bike; at full lock the cut is 10.5%, so a rider holding a long sweeper tucked is still faster than one sitting up, by a little less. Before October 15, 2026 the tuck came in at 72 km/h after 0.6 s and any steering over 0.3 sat the rider up at once; the play test read that as the rider standing up every time they turned. The renderer blends the rider toward a tucked pose by the same number, and combines it with the lean (see "Browser client").
- **Hills matter.** Grade speeds you up or slows you down. Vertical curvature changes the load on the tyres, so a crest goes light, and above about 170 km/h the big crest launches the bike.
- **Ramps throw the bike, and every landing is judged.** A ramp is a deck standing on the road, one of the track's `features` (see "Tracks"). A rider on a deck rides its surface: the deck's height, slope, and curvature add to the road's. Past the lip the bike flies with the lip's height and slope and no extra kick, so at 180 km/h a 16 m ramp that rises 0.8 m throws it 58 m along level road. The side of a deck is solid. A deck edge less than 0.1 m above the bike is ridden onto; a higher one is a wall, and running into it where the deck is over 0.2 m high while moving sideways faster than 1 m/s throws the rider. A gentler touch glances off, stops the sideways motion, and costs 2% of the speed. A rider who rides off the side of a raised deck drops off it. The deck's toe, sides, and lip are found along the bike's whole move in a tick, not only where it ends, and whichever it crosses first decides: a bike cannot cut through the corner of a deck just short of the lip, a rider who leaves by the side before the lip falls off the side instead of being launched, a flight that meets the deck from above lands on it where it meets it, and a bike that meets the side below the deck's surface, in the air or on the ground, hits it as a wall. A landing from a ramp or a crest is judged the same way: a downward impact against the surface harder than 11 m/s crashes the rider, and after a flight of 15 ticks or more, a quarter of a second, so does landing off the road or with sideways speed above 0.6 of the bike's steering limit. Any other landing costs 0.8% of the speed per m/s of impact, at most 8.8% short of a crash, and starts a wobble that grows with the impact and the sideways speed, sways the bike up to 0.8 m/s across the road, cuts its steering grip by up to 40%, and is gone within 0.9 s. Off a 16 m ramp at 180 km/h the bike comes down at 6.4 m/s and loses 5% of its speed.
- **Hazards lie on the road.** An oil slick, a rock, or a pothole is an entry in `RaceState.hazards` (see "Hazards as race state" below). Hazards are good to avoid, not must avoid: none is a crash on contact, and what one costs depends on speed and lean. A bike touches one when its footprint, moved along the straight path it takes in the tick, overlaps the hazard's on the ground; on the tick a bike lands, only the path after touchdown counts. Every hazard touched in a tick acts, in the order the path meets them, until one puts the bike in the air or on the ground. A bike in the air passes over, and a hazard the bike was already touching on the previous tick does not act again, though oil keeps the grip down for as long as the bike is on it. Oil takes 85% of the grip, and the share comes back at 1 per second once the bike is off the slick: the bars turn the bike's sideways speed toward the rider's input at the grip's share of the steer rate, the brakes keep 1 − 0.7 × the lost share and the drive 1 − 0.6 × it, and a bend pushes up to twice as hard, so a bike that was moving sideways keeps moving sideways and a bike in a bend slides wide. A short wobble sets in. Riding straight across at a steady line costs a few per cent of the speed and nothing else; holding 0.8 of the steer or more for 18 ticks (0.3 s) while more than half the grip is gone above 26 m/s (94 km/h) puts the rider down, a `hazard` crash that names the slick. Half the grip is back 0.35 s after the bike leaves the slick, so a quick tap of the bars on the slick or just past it is safe and only a held turn is not. A rock is a small road rock, 0.6 m across and 0.3 m high. Hit square above 14 m/s (50 km/h) it bumps the bike into the air: the bike leaves the road at 7% of its speed upwards on top of the road's own rise or fall, as a ramp launch does, so a rock on a climb throws it as high as one on the flat, loses 6% of its speed plus up to 10% more at 200 km/h, and is knocked sideways, the way it was already moving, by half its steering limit times the cube of its speed over 55.6 m/s (200 km/h). The landing rules for a jump then decide. Straight over at 130 to 180 km/h the bike is in the air for 0.5 to 0.75 s, lands wobbling, and rides on; from about 210 km/h it lands too far sideways and the rider is thrown; a bike steering at 0.7 of its limit or more is thrown from about 60 km/h, at half its limit from about 120 km/h. That landing crash is a `hazard` crash that names the rock. Slower than 14 m/s, or when the bike only clips the rock's edge by less than 0.2 m, it is a stagger: the bike keeps 40% of its speed (80% when clipping), is put beside the rock and pushed off it at 2.5 m/s, wobbles hard, and the rider flinches for ten ticks. A pothole is a jolt that costs 3% of the speed plus up to 3% more at 216 km/h, and a wobble of 0.3 that grows with how hard the bike is leaned and how fast it is going and is gone within a second; only a bike leaned past 85% of its steering limit above 48.6 m/s (175 km/h) is thrown, so a pothole ridden straight at any speed costs speed and a jolt and nothing more. A hazard crash costs the bike the same condition as a knockdown, 28 at 200 km/h. Passing within 0.8 m of a hazard's side above 20 m/s is a `nearMiss`.
- **Crashes separate rider and bike, and wear the bike out.** A hard hit throws the rider, who flies, bounces, slides, lies still for half a second, gets up, and is then on foot until they reach the bike, lift it, and remount (`thrown`, `lying`, `running`, `mounting`). The body keeps a share of the bike's speed that depends on the cause (half of it into a pole, 65% when knocked off) and loses 6 m/s² on the ground, so the fall is a function of speed: a rider thrown into a pole at 160 km/h is in the air and sliding for about 5 s and on their feet about 5.5 s after the impact, against the original's rider who tumbles and slides for at least 4.5 s and is standing at about 5.5 s; a knockdown at 100 km/h has the rider on their feet at 4.7 s; at 50 km/h at 3.1 s; and a tip-over at walking pace at 1.8 s. The bike stays where it fell. It slides on its side at 5 m/s², keeping 60% of its speed when the rider is knocked off, so after a fast knockdown it ends up nearer the body than the bike that stopped against a pole.

**On foot the rider is steered, not scripted.** In the `running` mode the rider's input moves the body: throttle runs up the road, brake runs back down it, and steer runs across it, so the controls mean the same thing they mean on the bike and the camera, which keeps facing along the road, never has to turn. The run tops out at 7 m/s in any direction (a diagonal is scaled so it is no faster), reaches that in a quarter of a second, and stops as fast. A control that was held when the rider went down does nothing on foot until it has been released: `throwRider` marks the throttle, the brake, and the steer as stale, and each clears on the first tick after the fall on which that control reads zero, so a player still holding the throttle from the ride does not walk the rider away from the bike and out of frame, and a fresh press moves them. A rider who touches nothing stands where they got up and can be arrested there; after 4 s (240 ticks) on their feet with no live input they walk back to the bike on their own at the run speed, and any fresh press takes over again. The first 18 ticks of `running` are the rise from the ground and take no input. The body is held inside the verges and between the start and the finish line; a rider cannot finish on foot. The rider remounts the moment the body comes within 0.7 m of the bike, whichever way they reached it, and `mounting` takes half a second before the bike moves. The pilot steers rivals and police through the same input: when it is called for a rider who is `running` it writes the unit vector from body to bike (`towardBike` in `rider.ts`) into the input and nothing else, and zeros while the rider is thrown, lying, or mounting, so the latch has cleared by the time an AI rider is on its feet and it runs straight back to its bike as it always did; in multiplayer a remote player's on-foot steering is the same five values as their riding. With the run at 7 m/s a knockdown at 50 km/h is back on the bike 5.3 s after the fall if the rider runs straight to it, one at 100 km/h 8.5 s, and the long walk after a 160 km/h crash is 13 to 16 s. A rider on foot can be knocked down again: a car or a bike moving faster than walking pace that reaches the body puts the rider back into the tumble, carrying a quarter of the vehicle's speed, and they are on their feet again about two seconds later. That is a `knockedDown` event, not a `crash`: the bike is not involved, so it costs no condition and counts as no crash, though a bike that does it is remembered as the last rider to hit them. A bike that runs over a body loses a tenth of its speed and bumps. Each crash also takes condition off the bike, which starts at 100. At 200 km/h that is 34 for traffic, 32 for a tree or pole, 30 for a hard landing or for running into another rider, 28 for a knockdown or a hazard, and 22 for leaving the track. The cost scales with the speed at impact over 200 km/h, held between a tenth and one and a quarter, so a crash at 100 km/h costs about 15 and a tip-over at walking pace about 3. At zero the bike is wrecked: the rider and bike slide to a stop as in any crash, are then moved onto the verge where traffic ignores them, and that rider's race is over with no fine. Three crashes into traffic at 200 km/h wreck a bike, and so do four knockdowns at that speed. Police bikes and riders who have already finished take no damage. A light side contact with a car only costs speed. A gentle bump into the nose of a car that is stopped or coming the other way leaves the bike up to 3 m/s and slides it sideways round the bumper at 3 m/s, towards the side the rider is steering or else the nearer side, so the bike and the car never hold each other still.

`BikeSpec` holds the numbers for one bike: power, drag, brakes, lateral speed, steer rate, curve push, lean scrub, and visual lean, with its class and price. Each rider carries their own (`RiderState.bike`), so the riding step, the pilot's corner speeds, and the lean the renderer draws all come from the bike that rider is on. `bikes.ts` has the nine bikes of the catalogue in `docs/design/game-design.md`, keyed by id. The Halden Wren 400 is the bike the numbers were measured on, against the original's starter bike. The Ostra Sesta 600 is exported as `streetBike` and is the bike the default race and most of the tests ride; its `curvePush` of 2.43 and `steerRate` of 5 stand in the same ratio to the Wren's 2.79 and 6 as before the retune, as does every other bike, and its `leanScrub` of 0.17 sits between the Wren's 0.2 and the Anvil's 0.14, so a heavy bike leaned over keeps more of its speed and needs the brake where a light one only needs to lean. Faster bikes are new specs, not new code.

`pilotInput` is the AI rider. It brakes for bends from the same physics: for every bend within its braking horizon it solves the speed at which the bend's push would take 80% of the bike's sideways speed at that speed, and plans 6.5 m/s² of braking to get there (the lean scrub works on it in the bend as on anyone, and it opens the throttle against it, so a rival rides a bend at about that speed and its brake-or-lift call comes from the same numbers the player's bike obeys); for every drop ahead it lifts, planning 3.5 m/s², to stay under 1.15 times the speed at which the bike would leave the road, so a heavy bike arrives at the Saddle at about 190 km/h and lands instead of crashing. It picks a lane around traffic and other riders, and throws punches and kicks at a rate set by its profile's aggression, after a truce of 7 s from the start so the pack sorts itself before the fists come out. A rival is paced by its gap to the player, rider 0, with the two numbers in `RaceState.pacing`: ahead of the player its throttle is capped so it holds no more than `topSpeed × (1 − leadEase × share)`, where `share` is the gap over 200 m clamped to 1, and it never brakes for pace; behind the player its corner skill is lifted by `catchUp × share`, never above 1, and it never exceeds its bike's top speed. The player, the police, busted and wrecked riders, and everyone once the player has finished, been busted, or been wrecked ride unpaced. `topSpeedOf(bike, tucked)` in `engine.ts` is the speed in top gear where the bike's thrust meets its drag and rolling loss, seated or tucked. It steers for where the bars will have taken it, aiming short of its lane by the distance its sideways speed still needs to ramp down, and it holds speed with a part throttle of about 0.6 that falls to nothing 0.75 m/s over its target; 1.5 m/s over, the brake takes over. A cop riding to the spot beside its quarry checks every lane it will cross for a car, not only the lane it is heading for; a same-way car beyond a downed rider's body does not count, because the cop stops at the body, but an oncoming car beyond it does, because it comes through. Its home is the same-way lane next to the centre line, and it goes back there whenever that lane is clear. When something blocks it, it takes the first clear lane in order of preference: the other same-way lane, then the oncoming lanes, so on a road with two lanes each way a rival behind a slow car passes in the outer lane and only crosses the centre line when both same-way lanes are blocked. A rider or a body overlapping the pilot along the road, within 2 m ahead or 4 m behind, blocks the lane it is in whatever its speed, so the pilot never steers into a bike alongside and a grid gets away in its own lanes instead of everyone converging on the home lane and leaning on each other. A lane it would have to cross to get there must not hold a threat due within 1.5 s, unless the bike is already in that lane, within 1 m of its centre; a bike beside a car does not steer through it to reach a clear lane on the far side. An oncoming lane takes more clearance to enter than to stay in: the pilot enters one only when the nearest oncoming car is more than 8 s of closing away, 12 s in the first 10 s of a race, and leaves one it is in when a car comes within 4.5 s, so a rider does not commit to the wrong side of the road and find the pack has closed the way back. When no clear lane can be reached it splits the seam between the lane it is in and the neighbouring lane, the same-way one if there is a choice, at the blocker's speed plus 8 m/s; a seam that straddles the centre line sits 0.6 m on the same-way side of it, which clears a truck's overhang. On a road with one lane each way nothing else is ever between two lanes: its own lane, then the oncoming lane, then the near side of the centre line. It judges a swing by the reach of the weapon in its hand and a kick by the reach of the boot, so a chain holder attacks from further across than a rider with fists. An unarmed rider next to an armed one prefers to kick. A rival can hold a grudge against one rider: when that rider is in reach the pilot goes for them instead of anyone nearer, on their side. A rival can also carry an `aggressionBoost` toward the player, rider 0. The pilot adds it to the rival's aggression whenever the rider it would strike is the player, with or without a grudge, so a boost above zero means more attacks on the player and one below zero means fewer. Toward everyone else the rival keeps its profile's aggression. The pilot rides round downed riders and ignores wrecked ones. The same code drives the title screen, the rivals, and the police. Its dice live in `RaceState`, so a race replays the same way whatever ran before it.

The pilot treats a hazard as a cost, not a wall, and chooses its line once, with hazards and traffic in the same scoring. It sees hazards 2.35 s plus 15 m ahead. While one is in sight, the candidates are the lanes, the lines just clear of either side of every hazard in sight (the hazard's half width, the bike's, and 0.45 m), the lines 0.3 m clear of either side of every car it is catching within sight, the line it chose last tick, following whatever blocks its own lane at that thing's speed when it can brake to that in time at 6.5 m/s², and for a cop the spot beside its quarry. A line is out when it is off the road (0.2 m inside the edge), on the side of a ramp it reaches first, or fails the lane tests above: traffic or a rider on the line, or a threat due within 1.5 s in a lane the move crosses. Every line left gets one score: its lane rank (1 per lane down the order of preference, 0.2 more between lanes, 5 more for following, -1 for a cop's pursuit spot), 0.05 per metre across, and a toll for what the move runs into. The toll comes from running the pilot's own steering tick by tick toward the line for up to 3.5 s: every hazard the bike would touch on the way, within the two half widths plus 0.1 m, costs 0.6 for a pothole, 1 for oil, and 2 plus 2 × (speed / 200 km/h)² for a rock, and every car it would reach while beside it costs 10, except the one a following line brakes for. A rock is also checked for the landing: the pilot takes the speed at which the bike, with the sideways speed the forecast gives it there plus the bend's push, and the rock's upset, would land under 85% of the slip that throws a rider, and rides the line at that speed; when braking at 6.5 m/s² cannot get it there before the rock, the line costs 10 more, as a crash. Oil is checked the same way for the slide: on a slick a bend pushes nearly twice as hard while the bars barely turn the bike, so the pilot takes the sharpest curvature from the slick's near edge to 0.6 s past it and rides a line that crosses the slick no faster than the speed at which that bend pushes the bike sideways at 1.2 m/s (across a turn-in slick on a 60° bend that is 76 km/h, and the bike slides 1.5 m instead of 3.2 m at the 139 km/h corner speed); when braking at 6.5 m/s² cannot get it there before the slick, the line costs 10 more. Oil on a straight costs no speed. So a pilot rides round turn-in oil when a lane is free and otherwise brakes before it and crosses it upright, and one squeezed onto the edge of a slick by a car or a rival can still slide into it. The line chosen last tick gets 0.15 off, so the pilot keeps a line until a better one is worth more than that or it stops being open, and does not flip sides in the middle of a move. A bump beats a truck: when the only open lines cross a hazard the pilot takes the hazard, and on a line that touches a rock it eases to 150 km/h or the landing speed, whichever is lower. When no line is open it splits the seam as above. While the bike is losing grip it never steers past 0.45, so it does not put itself down on oil. An unskilled rider sometimes does not see a hazard: the chance is 0.4 times its lack of skill, rolled once for the nearest hazard in sight from `RaceState.hazardDice`, a stream seeded for hazards alone so that hazards do not shift the dice for jumps and fights. Cops use the same chooser, so a cop rides round a hazard in a chase, and a hazard spawned during the race is seen exactly like one on the schedule.

The pilot takes ramps. It sees the next ramp 3.5 s and 10 m ahead and lines up on it when its chosen line passes within 3.6 m of the ramp, the deck's centre is at least 0.5 m inside the road edge, and it has time to reach the deck: 0.5 s plus 0.45 s for every metre it has to move across. It holds a line within the deck, off the centre by up to 1.2 m times its lack of skill, and caps its speed where the landing on level road would stay under 90% of the hard-landing limit. That is 327 km/h for the 16 m ramps and 287 km/h for the 18 m ones on Cinder Wash, so only a tucked Nera is ever held back. A rider that does not take a ramp keeps 1.2 m clear of its side, on whichever side keeps the bike on the road. An unskilled rider sometimes misjudges a ramp: the chance is 0.8 times its lack of skill, rolled once per ramp from the race's dice. It holds a line just off the edge of the deck, on the road side, and swerves onto it late, which can run it into the side and throw it. Before a crest that would launch it, within 0.6 s, the pilot straightens up, aiming to let its sideways speed run out, so it lands square.

Combat lives in `combat.ts`. A punch or kick lands on the nearest rider on the chosen side within about two metres, drains stamina, shoves them sideways, and stuns their steering briefly. The timings are tuned for snap, not for the original, whose measured 0.2 s wind-up the first play test called slow, and were shortened again on October 15, 2026 when the second play test still called them sluggish: a punch is fully out 4 ticks (0.07 s) after the press and the swing is over at 18 (0.30 s). The third play test, on October 15, asked for a quicker kick that rises off the seat first, the way the original's did: a kick lands 4 ticks (0.07 s) after the press and its swing is over at 15 (0.25 s), against 5 and 20 before. Its cooldown stays at 36, so a held kick key lands one kick every 0.6 s as before and fights last as long. A strike's `cooldown` is the shortest time from one press to the next. For fists it is 16 ticks, two short of the swing, so a press in the last two ticks of the follow-through starts the next punch at once with no return to neutral, and a held key lands a punch every 16 ticks and never faster; that is the old damage rate within a tenth, so the quicker arm does not shorten a fight. A kick cannot be chained into a follow-through, and a weapon with no hits left breaks when the chained swing starts. Every weapon's cooldown is longer than its swing, so a weapon always returns to neutral between swings. The renderer does not ease into a strike pose; it reads the swing's clock. `strikeFrame` in `game.ts` turns `attackTicks`, plus the frame's fraction of a tick, into a `reach` of 0 to 1: the arm or leg extends linearly over the wind-up so it is fully out on the tick the hit lands, holds for 4 ticks, and comes back over 5, so a punch is out at 4, held to 8, and seated by 13, and the 3 ticks of neutral before the 16-tick cooldown make mashing read as jabs. A kick has its own clock with two numbers: a `rise` off the seat and the leg's `reach`. The rise eases out over the first 2.5 ticks, so the rider is two thirds of the way up after one tick; the leg snaps out over the last 1.5 ticks before the hit, holds 2, and pulls in over 3; from tick 8 the rider drops back onto the seat, accelerating, and is seated at 13, two ticks before the swing ends. A kick from a tucked rider rises out of the tuck. Weapon swings and punches stay seated: the swing poses are whole-body poses authored seated, the stop sign's wind-up would hold a standing rider for half a second, and the original threw its swings from the seat. A weapon swing winds up into the windup pose over 3 ticks, holds it until 3 ticks before the hit, and then swings through to the strike pose on the landing tick, so the stop sign's 27-tick wind-up is 21 ticks of a visibly raised sign. The struck rider flinches: the renderer rolls the torso and head away from the hit by `hitStun`, which is 10 on the landing tick and falls to 0, so the reaction is instant and over in a sixth of a second. A rider whose stamina reaches zero is thrown. Strikes that come due on the same tick are all aimed before any of them lands, so two riders who swing at each other both connect. Cooldown and stun timers tick down before any strike lands, and every strike lands before any rider starts a new swing, so both are stunned for the same ten ticks and nobody starts a swing on the tick they were hit. Every rider remembers who last hit or touched them and when. A `crash` event carries `by`: that rider if it was within the last 60 ticks, otherwise −1, so a rider punched into a car is put down to the puncher. Rider-to-rider contact is sorted by where the two bikes were a tick earlier. If they were clear of each other along the road and already overlapping across it, the one behind ran in: a rear-end. Above a closing speed of 18 m/s it throws the rider behind, once per tick at most, and leaves them behind the bike they hit. Below that it is a bump that bleeds the speed forward: the rider behind drops to the speed of the bike ahead, less 0.5 m/s, and is held behind it, and the bike ahead is shunted on by 30% of the closing speed, up to 5.4 m/s, so a player who holds the throttle into the back of a slower bike is slowed to its pace rather than thrown. Before October 14, 2026 the line was 9 m/s, and a player who held the throttle off the grid was thrown into the pack in most starts. If they were clear across the road and overlapping along it, it is a side shove that pushes both bikes apart. If both gaps closed in the same tick, the one that closed last decides. A side shove costs each rider 0.9 m/s on a tick however many bikes touch them, and no more than 6 m/s in any second, so two bikes leaning on each other cannot hold each other still. The condition it costs and the speed on its `crash` event come from the speed the rider had at impact, not from the bike they ran into. Every pair in contact is read from the positions and speeds the riders had when the contact step began, and the pushes are added up before any is applied, so a three-bike pileup comes out the same whichever rider is listed first. A rider who runs hard into two bikes at once is thrown off the nearer one, and both of them are shunted forward. Traffic cars follow each other from the previous tick's positions. Each lane is its own sorted queue: a car follows the car ahead in its lane, never changes lane, and yields to a rider or a body in its lane. A car that has stopped for a rider stays stopped until the rider is out of its way; it does not creep forward. Remounting restores stamina to 55.

Weapons are numbers in `weapons.ts`, the same way handling is a `BikeSpec`. A rider holding one swings it when they press punch; kick stays a kick. Each strike has damage, shove, reach, timing, and a number of hits before the weapon breaks:

| Strike | Damage | Shove | Reach (along × across) | Hits | Lands after | Swing | Cooldown |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fist | 14 | 1.6 | 1.9 × 2.2 m |  | 4 | 18 | 16 |
| Boot | 22 | 3.2 | 1.9 × 2.2 m |  | 4 | 15 | 36 |
| Club | 28 | 3.8 | 2.2 × 2.6 m | 6 | 7 | 24 | 40 |
| Chain | 20 | 2.2 | 2.6 × 3.1 m | 8 | 7 | 24 | 38 |
| Crowbar | 22 | 2.8 | 2.2 × 2.6 m | 4 | 6 | 22 | 36 |
| Baton | 18 | 2.4 | 2.2 × 2.6 m | 12 | 6 | 22 | 36 |
| Cue | 15 | 1.8 | 3.0 × 3.4 m | 3 | 5 | 20 | 30 |
| Sign | 32 | 4.6 | 2.4 × 2.8 m | 3 | 27 | 44 | 50 |

The pool cue has the longest reach, 3.4 m across against the chain's 3.1 m, which is still short of a lane width. It swings again soonest of the weapons and snaps after three hits. The stop sign is the biggest hit and the slowest to come round again, 50 ticks against the club's 40, and a rider holding it has 85% of their bike's steering response.

`strikeTick` counts the ticks left in the swing when the hit lands, so a strike lands `ticks − strikeTick` ticks after the press. A weapon lands one to three ticks after a fist would, in the order cue, crowbar and baton, club and chain, and follows through for a tick or two longer; the swings and cooldowns are unchanged, so the cadence of an armed rival or a cop is what it was. The stop sign's swing is 44 ticks with a `strikeTick` of 17, so it lands 27 ticks (0.45 s) after the press, which is the shortest wind-up a rider 2 m across can still steer out of, and follows through for 17.

A kick that lands on an armed rider hands the weapon to the kicker if the kicker is unarmed. Who was armed is read when the strikes are aimed, so two riders who kick each other on the same tick end the same way whichever is listed first: the unarmed one takes the weapon and keeps it. A swing that lands on that tick still costs the weapon a hit, and if two unarmed riders kick the same holder the nearer one gets it. A thrown rider drops whatever they hold. Rivals start with the weapon in their profile and a full count of hits. The player starts with the weapon and the hits left that the race setup carries over from the last race.

Police are riders with the `police` role, so riding, contacts, combat, and the renderer treat them like anyone else. How many there are, how fast they chase, how far they see, how far past their post they keep chasing, and what a bust costs come from the race setup. `createRace` parks them on the verge, 1.1 m beyond the road edge, spread over the road's length: the first 1.5 km from the start, the last 1 km before the finish, and the rest evenly between, so a level's police cover the whole race however long the road is, and a single cop sits at 1.5 km. A parked cop sits still until a racer is alongside it or up to 35 m ahead of it, then pulls out, so the pack meets the first cop about forty seconds into a race, once fifteen riders have sorted themselves out, instead of chasing a patrol it may never catch. A cop that has pulled out and has nobody to chase patrols at 22 m/s. `police.ts` decides whom a cop chases: a downed racer whose body is within 60 m ahead comes first, then the nearest racer riding within the sight range, who is kept until they are 120 m away. Each cop remembers its `post`, the spot where it was parked. Once it is more than the setup's `beat` past that spot it chases nobody new, pulls onto the verge, and stays there; a cop riding to a downed racer finishes that first. The pilot rides alongside a riding target at up to the pursuit top speed, which a level sets below the top speed of the bikes raced there, and swings its baton. It rides to a downed rider's body no faster than that speed either. A chasing cop picks its lane around traffic the way a racer does, at the speed the chase calls for. It leaves that lane for the spot 1.5 m beside its quarry only when it is within 20 m of them and no car threatens that line, and for a downed rider's body only when no car nearer than the body does. A pilot gives a car that drives the other way its longer warning even when that car has stopped. A racer who is off the bike when a riding cop is within 4 m of their body is busted, except in the first 30 ticks after they are thrown: a rider who is running back or lifting the bike can be arrested at once, and one who has just come off cannot, so a cop riding alongside does not arrest a rider on the tick they fall. A cop riding alongside a rider who falls at 160 km/h brakes for the body, loses it for a moment as it passes behind, is overtaken by the body still flying at 29 m/s, picks it up again, and makes the arrest about a second after the fall; a cop that is 60 m back when a rider falls at 50 km/h arrives after the remount and gives chase instead. Their race ends with the setup's fine, and standings put busted and wrecked riders after everyone still racing, ordered by how far they got. A thrown rider drops their weapon at once, so each rider keeps `weaponAtThrow`, the weapon they held when they were last thrown. The `busted` event carries the body's `s` and `x` at the moment of the arrest and that weapon, all read before the rider is moved. The busted rider and bike are moved onto the verge and traffic stops braking for them, so a bust never blocks the road. A cop that is off its beat or within 80 m of the end of the track pulls onto the verge for the same reason: it crawls at 6 m/s until the bike is clear of the road edge and only then stops, because a bike that brakes to a halt first has no steering left to leave the lane with. A cop who is knocked down remounts with a fresh baton. Police are not in the standings and cannot finish.

### Hazards as race state

The compiled track only holds the schedule. `createRace` hands every scheduled placement to `spawnScheduledHazards` in `hazards.ts`, which calls the spawn function for its kind: `spawnOil`, `spawnRock`, or `spawnPothole`, each `(state, track, spot)` where `spot` is `{ id, s, x, command }`. A spawn appends a `Hazard` to `RaceState.hazards` with its id, kind, size, position, the tick it appeared, and `command`, which is null for the schedule. It refuses, returning undefined, a spot that is off the road, overlaps a ramp's deck, or reuses an id already in the race, so a hazard id is unique in a race and its index in the list never changes. Riding, the pilot, the cops, and the renderer read only `RaceState.hazards`, never the schedule, so a hazard spawned between ticks is ridden into, avoided, and drawn exactly like one placed by the track. The `crash` event carries `hazard`, the hazard's id, kind, and command, or null for a crash with another cause; a touch that does not crash is a `hazard` event with the effect (`slip`, `stagger`, `bump`, or `jolt`), and a near miss carries the hazard or null for a car. Each rider keeps the indices of the hazards it touched on the last tick in `hazardContacts`, and the rock that bumped it into the air in `bumpedBy` until it lands, so the landing crash can name it. The `land` event carries the touchdown `s` and `x`.

This is the surface the race director builds on. A director command stamped with a tick, applied at the start of `stepRace` as in the lane-change experiment on `feat/race-director-events`, calls `hazardSpawners[kind]` with the command's id as `command`, and every event the hazard causes names that command. `stepRace` takes no director argument yet.

## Race setup and cast

`createRace` takes a `RaceSetup`: the track and seed, the player (a profile, a bike, and the weapon and hits they carry in), the rivals for this race (each a profile and a bike, with an optional `grudge` rider index and an optional `aggressionBoost` toward the player), the police settings (count, pursuit top speed, sight range, beat, fine, the officer's profile, and the bike they ride), the pacing rule (`leadEase` and `catchUp`), and the grid order. Rider 0 is always the player, the rivals follow in the order given, and the police come last; a grudge index counts in that order. With `playerLast` the rivals fill the grid one to a lane across the whole road, rows 7 m apart, and the player has a row to themselves behind them. With `playerFirst` the player is on the front row. On a road with one lane each way the grid is two abreast and straddles the centre line; with two lanes each way it is four abreast. The player's row starts in the same-way lane next to the centre line.

`validateSetup(setup)` returns a list of typed problems: more than fourteen rivals, a rival listed twice, or an entrant with no bike. `createRace` calls it and throws one error carrying every message, so a caller at a boundary validates first.

`defaultSetup(track, seed)` is the race the tests and the title screen use: the player on the Sesta, five rivals on their level-3 bikes, one cop at 48 m/s with a 40 m sight range, a 1,500 m beat, and a $1,500 fine, level-3 pacing, and the player last on the grid.

`cast.ts` holds the sixteen rivals and the two officers from the design: name, skill, aggression, home region, the weapon they carry and the level they carry it from, a bike for each of the five levels, and their colours. `rivalAt(rival, level)` turns one into a setup entry. From level 4 an unarmed rival with aggression of 0.5 or more starts with a club. Their lines, the field draw, and everything else about the career live in `packages/career`.

A profile's `paint` and `jacket` are indices into the eighteen entries of `palette.ts`, which gives the bike, jacket, and helmet colour for each rider and a trim colour where there is one: the gold stripe on Juno Kade and the blue panels on the white police bikes.

## Tracks

`packages/track` compiles a `TrackPlan` into a `Track`. A plan is a list of sections, each with a length, a total turn in degrees, and a climb in metres, plus its region, its level, its traffic per kilometre, and the positions where a roadblock may stand. Compilation eases curvature and height through each section, samples the centre line every metre, and scatters trees, poles, rocks, and chevron signs from the plan's seed. The simulation and the renderer read the same compiled track.

A plan also says how many lanes the road has each way, `lanesEachWay`, 1 or 2, and how wide a lane is. The road's half width is `lanesEachWay × laneWidth + shoulderWidth`; the compiled `Track` lists the lane centres as `sameWayLanes` and `oncomingLanes`, positive x for the race's direction and negative for oncoming, each from the centre line outward. Traffic spawns in every lane, the plan's `trafficPerKilometre` cars per direction shared between that direction's lanes, and the slower cars favour the outer lane. The start is kept clear: no same-way car spawns between 70 m before the line and 260 m past it, and no oncoming car between 70 m before and 300 m past, so the oncoming lanes the grid stands in meet their first car once the pack is moving. Riding, props, the police verge, and the renderer all read the half width, so a wider road is wider everywhere.

There are five roads, one per region. Breakwater Run, Cinder Wash, and Slag Row have two lanes each way; Hollow Pine Road and Ridge Road have one. `docs/design/tracks.md` has their sections and the reasoning.

| Road | id | Region | Level 1 | Level 5 | Lowest launch |
| --- | --- | --- | --: | --: | --: |
| Breakwater Run | `breakwater-run` | Salt Coast | 8,500 m | 13,100 m | 169 km/h |
| Cinder Wash | `cinder-wash` | Cinder Flats | 8,850 m | 13,450 m | 154 km/h, the Main Wash |
| Hollow Pine Road | `hollow-pine-road` | Kettle Woods | 8,050 m | 12,650 m | 162 km/h, at level 5 |
| Slag Row | `slag-row` | Lowtown | 8,500 m | 13,100 m | 152 km/h, the rail crossing |
| Ridge Road | `ridge-road` | Stonepeak | 8,550 m | 13,150 m | 162 km/h, the Saddle |

The level-1 lengths follow the original game's level-1 roads, which measure 8.0 to 8.9 km. A level-1 race takes the AI pilot 3 to 3.6 minutes alone on an empty road on the Wren and 3.2 to 4.1 minutes (median 3.7) in a full field with traffic.

A road file in `tracks/` exports its level-1 plan, the four blocks of sections that levels 2 to 5 append, and a `Road` that ties them to the road's roadblock spots. `roadAtLevel(road, level)` builds a level's plan: the level-1 sections followed by the blocks up to that level, 1,150 m more road each time, one more car per kilometre, the id `slag-row-3`, and the same seed. `roadblocks` lists the spots that exist on the road at that level and is empty below level 3. A level can list more spots than it uses, and nothing in the simulation reads them yet.

A plan also lists `features`, things placed on the road. The only kind is a fixed ramp (`kind: "jump"`) with an id, the level it appears from, a toe position `s`, a lateral position `x`, and a length, width, and rise. The deck height is `rise × u²`, with `u` running from 0 at the toe to 1 at the lip, so every ramp in the game ends at a lip slope of `2 × rise / length`. `compileFeatures` in `features.ts` places them in `Track.features`. Each reserves the 80 m before its toe and a landing corridor after its lip, its level flight at 306 km/h plus 25 m, and no other feature may overlap that stretch; it must start at least 300 m after the start, end before the finish, and not hang over the road edge. The compiled features are indexed in 32 m cells, and `supportAt(track, s, x, out)` fills `out` with the deck height, slope, and curvature under a point and which feature it is, or the bare road. It is the one surface query: the riding step and the renderer both read it, so a bike drawn on a deck is pitched to the slope the simulation rides. `crossRamps(track, fromS, fromX, toS, toX, out)` sweeps a tick's move against the ramps' footprints and gives the share of the move at which it enters and leaves the first one it meets, and whether through a side, and `nextJumpFrom` finds the next ramp by binary search over `Track.features`, which are sorted along the road. `docs/design/tracks.md` lists every ramp and the rules for placing one.

A plan's `hazardsPerKilometre` places oil slicks, rocks, and potholes from the road's seed: `placeHazards` in `hazards.ts` fills `Track.hazards` with `HazardPlacement`s (a stable id such as `hazard-7`, the kind, the level it appears from, `s`, and `x`), and `hazardSizes` gives each kind's footprint and height. Each placement also names its setting: where a bend turns in, its apex, its exit, just over a crest, or a straight where riders pass traffic. The schedule is stable across levels, keeps hazards off the start, the finish, and every ramp's reservation, keeps every hazard on the lanes, and only checks that a hazard can be seen from 25 m, so it is never hidden until it is under the wheel. No rock lies where the road from 10 m before it to 40 m past it curves at 0.0015 or more, and a rock where it curves more gently lies at a lane's edge, clear of a bike on the lane's centre. Traffic ignores hazards: cars drive over them unchanged, and only riders meet them. Test plans set `hazardsPerKilometre` to 0. `docs/design/tracks.md` has the rules and every road's hazards.

`roads.ts` is the registry: `roads`, `roadIds` in career order, `regionOf(roadId)`, and `trackFor(roadId, level)`, which compiles each road and level once and returns the same `Track` afterwards.

`regions.ts` holds each region's name and prop mix. The prop mix weights trees, poles, rocks, and chevrons in the scatter, so Kettle Woods is dense with trees and has no poles and Cinder Flats is nearly bare. A region's colours and light are the renderer's business and live in `apps/web/src/game/looks.ts`. The renderer decides what a prop looks like per kit: a tree is a round street tree in a city and a pine on open ground, and a rock is a barrier block in a city. Rocks are scattered from their own seeded stream, so the scenery of the part of a road the player already knows is the same at every level.

The track tests compile every road at every level. They check the lengths, the lowest launch speed against the crest tables, that only the Main Wash and the rail crossing launch below 160 km/h, and that every roadblock spot is on a straight with 250 m behind it and 150 m ahead. They check each level's ramps by id, and that every ramp is on a straight from 40 m before its toe to the end of its landing corridor, with no crest in that stretch that launches below 200 km/h and no roadblock spot within 100 m of it. They also pin the first 4,250 m of the level-1 Ridge Road, the original track, by digest (curvature, heights, centre line, crests, every prop, and the rocks by themselves), and check that a longer lead-in changes the trees and leaves the 35 rocks where they were.

## Career

`packages/career` owns every career rule: money, levels, qualification, the shop, the field draw, rival memory, season points, story flags, settlement, and the save format. Like the simulation it is plain data and plain functions, with no DOM, clock, or unseeded randomness. React renders what it returns and never changes a rule; if the career should behave differently, change `packages/career`. It reads the cast, the bikes, and `RaceSetup` from `packages/sim` and the road ids and levels from `packages/track`. It never looks up or compiles a track: the caller passes one in.

`CareerState` is the save. It holds cash, level, the bike ridden and every bike owned, the carried weapon, the roads qualified at each level with the best place, the record, season points, what each rival remembers, story flags, the three newest board notes, the active-race record, the last settled result, and the game-over flag. Bike condition is not in it, because bikes are repaired between races. Every function returns a new state and leaves the old one alone, so a screen can ask what a bill would do before the player agrees to it.

| File | Owns |
| --- | --- |
| `tables.ts` | The numbers from `docs/design/game-design.md`: prizes, repairs, fines, police per level, shop stock, road lengths, trade-in and credit rates |
| `state.ts` | `CareerState` and `newCareer` |
| `field.ts` | `fieldFor`, `setupFor`, and `roadsFor`: who races, the `RaceSetup`, and which roads are open |
| `outcome.ts` | The race log and `outcomeOf` |
| `settle.ts` | `startRace`, `settleRace`, `quitRace`, `resumeCareer`, and the `Ledger` |
| `result.ts` | `LastResult`: the stored summary of the last settled race |
| `memory.ts`, `season.ts` | Rival memory and the season board |
| `shop.ts`, `story.ts`, `lines.ts` | The garage, the story beats and the envelope, and every line the cast and the officers say |
| `save.ts` | `serialiseCareer` and `parseCareer` |

A career race runs like this. `setupFor(career, roadId, seed, track)` draws the fourteen rivals, puts each on their bike and weapon for the level, sets grudges and aggression bonuses from memory, and adds the level's police and pacing. `startRace` writes the active-race record and clears the last result. The simulation clears its events every tick, so the runner calls `recordTick(log, state, setup)` after each `stepRace`. The log counts knockdowns, stolen weapons, hits, and busts, with the tick and the kilometre of each, and freezes the result at the tick the player's `finish`, `busted`, or `wrecked` event fires. Nothing after that tick changes it. The arrest's position and the confiscated weapon come from the `busted` event. `outcomeOf` returns the frozen result, or a quit if the player has not settled yet. `settleRace(career, outcome)` applies it once: prize with the repeat-run rule, repair or fine, the envelope, the debt check with repossession and game over, qualification, level-up, the ending, season points, memory, and the carried weapon. It clears the active-race record in the same step and returns a `Ledger` for Results.

**Race identity.** Every started race has a `raceId`, the career's count of starts. `startRace` puts it in the active-race record, `setupFor` puts it in the setup, and the race log stamps it into the outcome. `settleRace` accepts an outcome only when its `raceId`, road, level, and seed match the active race, so a result from an earlier race cannot pay again, even on the same road with the same seed. A refusal is a value, not a throw: the `Settlement` has `settled: false`, a null ledger, the career unchanged, and a `reason` of `noActiveRace`, `gameOver`, or `wrongRace`.

**Quitting.** `quitRace(career, outcome)` takes the live outcome from `outcomeOf` at the tick of the quit. Every rival is classified where they are at that tick, the player is placed behind everyone still racing and counts as wrecked, and the full repair bill is charged with no ad offer. Season points, memory, and board notes from the part of the race that was ridden all count. A player who quits after their race has already settled gets that settled result.

**Reloading.** `resumeCareer` is what the web app calls on a loaded save. If the save holds an active-race record, the race itself is gone, so it settles an empty outcome as a wreck at the full bill, once: no places, no points, and nothing remembered. It adds Vic's note to the board. With no active-race record it returns a `noActiveRace` refusal and the save unchanged.

**The last result.** Settlement stores `lastResult` in the career: the `Ledger` and a summary with the race's `raceId` and settlement tick, a row for each racer (name, bike, place, fate, finish tick or metres left, and prize), the incidents with each rival, the arrest (officer, their line, kilometre, confiscated weapon, fine), and the wreck (bike and kilometre). It is in the save, so a refresh during the Finish animation or on Results still has every number those screens show. The next `startRace` clears it.

**The save.** `parseCareer` checks every field and range and returns an error value for a bad save; it does not throw, whatever the text holds. The error has a kind and the path of the first field that failed. `invalid` is a value of the wrong kind or out of range, or a road or racer the game does not have. `inconsistent` is a save that contradicts itself: an active race on a bike other than the one ridden, at another level, with the wrong `raceId`, in a career that is over, or beside a stored result; a stored result that is not the last race started; a season or memory stamp later than the number of starts; or a bike ridden that is not in the garage. Reading and writing `localStorage` is the web app's job.

**The board.** Notes from a race are ordered by the tick they happened at, newest first, ahead of older notes, and the board keeps three. A repossession or a new bike at the garage happens at settlement and goes on top.

The design left some things open. These are the choices made here:

- A knockdown is a `crash` whose `by` names the other rider, whatever the cause except `rider`, so a rival punched into a car counts and a rival who runs into the back of the player does not. The same holds the other way: a rival who punches the player into traffic is put down for the knockdown. The log keeps the kilometre of the last knockdown each way, and a rival who knocked the player down gets a `knockedPlayerDown` board note.
- A rival placed ahead of the player won by a wheel when the player finished within 30 ticks after them. A rival placed behind lost by a wheel when they finished within 30 ticks after the player, or were still riding within 25 m at settlement.
- A rival's aggression bonus applies toward the player at any size and either sign. The grudge flag only decides whether the rival prefers the player as a target.
- The save version is 2. Version 1 saves carried memory and season rows for ten rivals, and the parser requires a row for each of the sixteen, so a version-1 save is rejected as `wrongVersion` rather than read with six rivals missing. No save had been written by a released build when `raceId` and `lastResult` were added at version 1.
- Police ride the Sesta at levels 1 to 3, the Meridian at level 4, and the Nera at level 5, so the bike is always faster than the level's pursuit speed.
- Selling is `sellBike`: the player gets the trade-in value and rides the Gutter Pup. It is the only shop action open to a player in debt.
- The envelope can be taken until the player's first start on Breakwater Run at level 2 or the move to level 3, whichever comes first.
- Once an ending is earned, no further career race starts.

## Browser client

`apps/web/src/game` builds the world from the compiled track: chunked road and terrain meshes, instanced scenery and traffic, a chase camera, and synthesized audio whose engine note follows the simulation's revs, from 52 Hz at idle to 300 Hz at the redline, and dips for the cut at every upshift. The loop steps the simulation at 60 Hz and draws interpolated poses at the display's refresh rate.

Picture quality comes first. The game renders at the screen's native pixel ratio, up to 2, and paces itself at 60 frames per second; on a faster display it only tries the display's rate after ten calm seconds at full quality, and falls back to 60 if more than about one frame in ten then misses, waiting a minute, then two, then four before trying again. It never lowers quality to reach more than 60. The governor never sees the pacer's choices: it judges every animation callback the browser delivers, with the main-thread work of the last frame drawn, never the gaps between the frames the pacer presents. A callback counts as late when its gap passes 1.4 times the frame budget at the pace, 23 ms at 60 and 47 ms at 30, which on a 120 Hz screen at 60 is three vsyncs and on a 60 Hz screen two, and each callback weighs as at most one frame, so a 120 Hz screen's callbacks count as half a frame each at 60. The governor in `governor.ts` moves quality on two kinds of lateness. A sustained trickle of late frames, more than about one in ten, moves quality down one rung at most every five seconds (`late-budget`). A burst moves it faster (`late-burst`): the governor counts, in each second, the late frames whose own main-thread work took under 10 ms, so the lateness came from the GPU and a cheaper picture will help; two seconds in a row with one such frame in ten, or one second with half, steps down after a one-second settle, two rungs at once for the half. A CPU-bound stretch, where lowering the picture would not help, only ever meets the slow rule. A third rule watches the browser's delivery (`under-rate`): every two seconds it counts the vsyncs the callbacks covered, a callback more than one and a half refresh periods after the last covering the vsyncs it spans, and two GPU-bound windows in a row under 97 percent of 60 a second step down one rung. A stall is a long gap and belongs to the lateness rules; this one sees callbacks that come a little too slowly, and the 60 Hz floor means a refresh reading that is off, or stale after a move, cannot make a healthy screen look short. Judging callbacks rather than presented frames means no cadence the pacer picks, right or wrong, can lower quality. Before October 17 it judged the presented frames, and review found pacer misjudgements that each became a false quality drop: a 120 Hz screen read as 122, a window that straddled a move between screens, and pacing from the observed callback rate, which flapped on callbacks jittering by a millisecond and misread a 240 Hz screen whose timestamps rounded to 3 and 5 ms. Safari gives whole-millisecond timestamps and, on the MacBook Air in the October 16 play test, slipped to about 56 frames a second as runs of 17 and 18 ms intervals, so no single frame passed 23 ms and the other rules saw nothing while the player saw judder for the whole race; a count over a window is exact however the timestamps are rounded, and a steady 60, or a 120 Hz screen paced to 60, delivers 99 to 100 percent and never trips it. When the lowest rung still slips, the governor holds a steady 30 (`steady-30`): the loop keeps interpolating between simulation ticks and presents every nth vsync, n being the refresh over the pace rounded to a whole number, which reads smoother than an uneven 50-odd. The pacer counts animation callbacks against the confirmed refresh rather than comparing timestamps with deadlines worked out from it: a callback more than one and a half refresh periods after the last counts as the vsyncs it spans, and the frame is presented once the count reaches n, after which the count starts again, so a missed vsync starts a new cadence rather than rushing the next frame to catch up. After a move to another screen the cadence stays the old screen's until the meter confirms the new rate, up to about three seconds of uneven frames, which cannot touch quality. Deadlines from the measured rate juddered: a 120 Hz screen read as 122 at pace 60 presented 9 and 24 ms apart, and the 24 ms frames set off a late burst three seconds in. On a screen whose refresh is not a multiple of 30 that is the nearest even rate, 25 fps at 75 Hz and 28.8 at 144 Hz. The refresh rate is read each second from the animation callbacks themselves: the mean of the gaps that span one vsync, which ignores dropped callbacks and averages out Safari's whole-millisecond timestamps, rounded to a whole hertz rather than snapped to a list, so a 100 Hz screen paces 30 as every third refresh. The meter starts from 60 and takes any reading, the first included, only when two consecutive seconds agree, so moving the window from a 144 Hz screen to a 75 Hz one is followed, but a quarter-second burst of fast callbacks or a single rough second is not; taking rises at once, or trusting the first reading, let one such burst at load hold a 60 Hz screen at 100 for seconds and cost two rungs. Until the rate has been confirmed once, about two seconds in, the governor does not count delivered vsyncs. If four readings pass without two agreeing, the meter confirms the lowest of them: Safari slipping in uneven runs read 60 and 66 by turns and would otherwise have left the frame-rate rule off for the whole race, and the lowest reading never blames a healthy screen for missing frames it was never going to show. Later changes still need two agreeing readings. Nothing reads below 60, because Safari slipping to 56 must still count as missing frames on a 60 Hz screen. The first version took the shortest single interval, and one early timestamp read the M4 Pro's 120 Hz screen as 144, which paced 30 as 28.8 with uneven gaps. Leaving 30 counts as a retry of the lowest rung, so it needs five calm minutes and happens at most once. Settings has Frame rate: Auto is the governor, 60 never locks to 30 and never paces above 60, and 30 holds 30 with the quality rungs still judged against a 30 frame budget; changing it is recorded as `rate-setting`, and every quality record carries the mode. The rungs go from cheap to visible: the 4096 shadow map halves to 2048, the bloom's first mip drops from the CSS size to half of it, the scene target's 4x multisampling turns off, then the pixel ratio comes down in steps of 0.1 to a floor of three quarters of native, 1.5 on a Retina screen. Multisampling goes before resolution because it costs as much: on the M4 Pro the uncapped stress race took 6.2 to 7.5 ms a frame at ratio 2 with it and 4.6 ms without, the same as ratio 1.5 with it, and ratio 2 without it looks sharper than 1.5 with it. Twenty calm seconds raise quality one rung (`calm-recovery`), but the governor remembers every rung it has stepped down from in the page's life, across ladder rebuilds when the window moves to a screen with another pixel ratio, even one that shortens the ladder for a while: climbing back into one needs five calm minutes (`calm-retry`), and after a second failure it does not try again. A browser that names a modest GPU, `Apple GPU` (Safari hides the model), a base Apple M chip without Pro, Max, or Ultra, Intel, Mali, Adreno, or PowerVR, starts at the rung without multisampling and counts the rungs above it as failed once (`device-start`). Each change that resizes the canvas reallocates every render target, which cost 50 to 80 ms on the MacBook Air in the October 16 play test, so fewer, larger steps are better than many small ones. That cost lands outside the measured main-thread work, so it would look like a GPU burst: the callbacks until two frames after any resize or rung change have been drawn are left out of the governor's accounting, and dragging the window never lowers quality. Hiding the tab does the same: the governor drops its part-filled windows on each visibility change and skips the callbacks until two frames after it have been drawn, because the title's attract race keeps running and its first frame back would otherwise carry the whole hidden gap. The frame meter, turned on in Settings or with `?fps`, shows frame rate, the raw frame time and worst frame (not the 0.1 s clamped value the simulation steps by), the simulation time that clamp threw away, the pixel ratio, the rung, the pace, the detected refresh, draw calls, triangles, and the last quality change with its reason; `?ratio=` pins the pixel ratio, `?rung=` pins a rung of the ladder, and `?hz=` pins the detected refresh for measurement. Before October 15, 2026 the governor judged frames against the display's refresh while the ratio was above 1, so on a 120 Hz Retina laptop it walked Slag Row down toward ratio 1 to chase 120 frames per second and rarely came back; that read as a nosedive in resolution and as choppy, because every step resized every render target.

Play tests on other machines are recorded, with the tester's consent, by `apps/web/src/diagnostics`. The hub there is created before the game loads and owns the session (a UUID per page load, the build's commit and dirty flag stamped by `vite.config.ts`, and the device the tester picked), the device-local settings under `rushline.settings.v1`, the recorder, and the uploader. In the frame loop the recorder only writes numbers into preallocated rings; JSON and network work happen in a 30 second batch outside it, and a period with no frames and no events sends nothing, so a tab left on a menu or in the background stops filling the store with empty batches. The game numbers the canvas's drawing buffer: every resize is a new generation, each frame notes the generation it drew, and at the start of the next frame a mismatch is recorded as a frame a resize cleared before it was presented, which is how the black flash fixed on October 16, 2026 showed up. `packages/telemetry` holds the wire contract: `limits.ts` with no dependencies for the browser, and `schema.ts`, the Effect schema `POST /api/telemetry` (`apps/web/src/routes/api/telemetry.ts`, `apps/web/src/server/telemetry.ts`) validates against before an injected store acknowledges the batch. `vite.config.ts` injects a JSONL store for `vite dev` and `vite preview`, so `node:fs` never reaches the Worker; a store for the deployed Worker is not built yet. `tools/perf/README.md` has the play-server command and the session CLI.

`?stress` starts a fixed worst-case race the moment the game loads and puts the player on the pilot, in the production build too: Slag Row at level 5 with the full field, seed 31, eight police, rivals at their most aggressive toward the player, and pacing that holds the pack around the player. Ticks 2640 to 3240 are the measured window: ten or eleven rivals ahead in view, about thirty cars within reach, a cop chasing within 25 m of the player, and fights at the camera. `?stress=reference` is the ordinary Slag Row level 1 race with the same seed. Both are built in `apps/web/src/career/bench.ts` from public setup functions and change no rule. `?prof`, which `?stress` implies, records each frame's CPU time per part (sim, riders, fleet, camera, world, matrices, the shadow map, and each render pass) and the draw calls and triangles per pass into preallocated arrays, read through `globalThis.__rushlineProfile`; `?prof=gpu` adds timer queries per pass, which ANGLE on Metal answers with numbers too large to trust. `tools/perf/measure.ts` drives headless Chrome through it; its README has the commands.

Starting a race does not compile shaders. The pre-race screen asks the game to `prepare` its plan: the game builds the road, the field, and the traffic while the screen is up, compiles the new materials with `compileAsync`, which uses `KHR_parallel_shader_compile` where the browser has it, and then draws the start grid once behind the screen. Start stays inactive, labelled as loading the road, until that finishes, because starting on half-compiled shaders would block the first frame until they were done. Start then only begins the countdown. Every load builds the new world and actors before it disposes the old ones, so a program the two share is never dropped and compiled again, and the title's attract race compiles in the background while the loop holds its last frame. The pipeline compiles with three's synchronous `compile`, which hands the programs to the driver, and then polls `KHR_parallel_shader_compile` itself over `renderer.info.programs` rather than using `compileAsync`. three's poller reads the properties of every material it compiled and cannot be stopped, so disposing one of them, or losing the context, made its next poll throw, and a poll that never finished held whatever waited on it. The game's poller only reads the renderer's live program list, ignores a program three has already destroyed, and stops when asked, so old worlds and actors are disposed as soon as the new ones are built and teardown is immediate. A newer load stops the older poll, and a compile that is still pending after five seconds, or when the WebGL context is lost, stops polling and counts as ready; the race then warms up the old synchronous way, a stall rather than a Start button that never comes back. The pre-race screen enables Start once preparation ends, whether it succeeded or not. A replaced world belongs to the build that replaced it and is disposed exactly once, by that build's release. Before October 16, 2026 the Start click did all of this at once: about 200 ms on the M4 Pro and 300 ms on the Air.

Nothing is drawn that the fog has already hidden. The road, kerb, walk, terrain, and bank chunks are 200 m long and only the chunks within `fogFar` plus 200 m ahead of the player and 260 m behind are visible, the same reach as the city's chunks; the camera's far plane stays at 6 km for the sky, the plain, and the mountains, which do not take fog. Traffic is one instanced mesh per model part, and each frame only the cars within that reach ahead and 40 m behind are written into it, so a road's hundred-odd cars cost the vertex shader only when they can be seen. On Slag Row, the heaviest road, that took a frame at ratio 2 from about 900 draw calls and 5.7 million triangles to about 600 and 2.9 million. The frame there is bound by the CPU side of three's draw submission, not the GPU, so draw calls are the number to watch.

Every surface is drawn in two tones. `cel.ts` makes the materials: a toon ramp with one hard step, a hard rim strip on the side facing the sky's light, and a hard highlight on glossy paint. The shadow tone is the ambient light and the lit tone is ambient plus the one directional light, so a look states both tones as the colours they should reach on screen. `post.ts` draws the frame through a fixed chain: the scene, ink outlines found from a normal and depth pass, bloom on anything brighter than a lit wall, a colour fringe and vignette, then output. Sky, clouds, cables, and far ground sit on a second layer the outline pass does not see. One 4096 shadow map follows the camera.

`looks.ts` holds a look per region: sky, fog, the two tones, light directions, outline colour, wall and road colours, window and lamp colours, bloom, and whether the road is wet. A look also picks a kit. Salt Coast is a city at dusk and Lowtown is a wet city at night. Cinder Flats is open desert at dawn, Kettle Woods is open forest at night, and Stonepeak is an open ridge at dusk. The city kit in `city.ts` lines both sides of the road with buildings generated from the track's seed: shopfronts, towers, awnings, balconies, blade signs, billboards, and cables. It builds them in 200 m chunks around the player. Building fronts stand at the distance where the simulation crashes a rider who leaves the road, so what looks solid is solid. The open kit keeps the terrain, trees, rocks, and a ring of mountains, and `roadside.ts` dresses the edge of the road from the track's seed: cut banks of faceted rock, a marker post with a red reflector every 28 m, boulders, warning signs, and on Hollow Pine Road a dense tree line. Each look says in its `roadside` how much of each it gets. Banks, posts, trees, and boulders stand at the same distance as the city's building fronts, just past the verge where the simulation throws a rider who leaves the road, so nothing the renderer adds can be ridden into without the simulation knowing. The banks are merged per 200 m chunk; everything else is instanced. `ground.ts` places things on the ground: the terrain lift beside the road, the kerb, and the instancing of scattered props. `textures.ts` draws every texture on a canvas at load: facades, shopfronts, the sign atlas, the road with its lines, paving, and the sky. The road texture follows the lane count: solid edge lines at the outer lane edges, the double centre line, a dashed divider between the two same-way lanes where a road has two lanes each way, and a band of wheel wear down every lane centre. A tile is 24 m long and 512 texels across on a one-lane road, 1024 on a two-lane road, so a line is about the same number of texels wide on both.

`hazards.ts` draws `RaceState.hazards` every frame, not the track's schedule, so a hazard spawned mid-race appears at once. Each kind has one instanced mesh per part, sized from `hazardSizes`: oil is a glossy black slick: a dark lobed pool, a faint ring of dark teal, plum, and olive sheen, and a pale band of reflected sky across its far end, all lit like the road and nothing that glows; a pothole is a near-black hole whose far inner wall catches the light, ringed by a broken asphalt edge lighter than the road and a low raised lip 5 cm high, so its edge shows even when the hole is seen nearly edge-on; a rock is a small faceted stone the size of its footprint in the region's rock colour with a soft contact shadow, on every road. Hazards are drawn at their real size and are not made to stand out: seeing one late is part of the game. Only hazards from 40 m behind the camera's rider to the fog plus 200 m ahead are written into the instances, and the buffers are rewritten only when that set or a hazard's position changes. A `jolt` or a `bump` kicks the bike's suspension sag, and a `stagger`, a `bump`, or a hazard crash near the camera throws up road dust. The DEV ride flags `?hazard=<id>&before=<m>&kmh=<speed>` start the player that far before a hazard, on its line, at that speed; `&straight` holds full throttle and steers to keep the bike on the hazard's line through bends; `&clear` moves traffic out of the way from the start to 400 m past the hazard; and `&hold=near&holdafter=<m>` freezes the frame when the player is that close to it, alongside `hold=hazard` for the first touch.

`ramps.ts` builds each ramp once per road from the same `supportAt` surface the simulation rides, so the deck the bike is drawn on is the deck it rides. The deck is a dark steel plate 0.14 m thick with a yellow edge, grip bars, and a hazard band at the lip, and a raised steel kerb along each side. Under it the ramp is an open frame, a rail on the road, posts every 2 m, and cross-braces, in front of a black centre wall and underside, so a rider beside it sees daylight between the posts and a void under the deck rather than a painted strip. The lip has a striped face over a 0.7 m recess, legs, and a cross-brace. The ramp material has no rim light: the rim strip lit the deck where it is seen at a grazing angle and made it read as a glowing strip. Cyan is kept to a thin line along each kerb and a brighter line at the lip. Two striped posts 2.9 m tall with amber lamps stand 0.3 m outside the lip corners, tall enough to show over a lined-up rider's back from 60 m, and yellow chevrons on the road count down the last 72 m. A soft contact shadow, darker where the deck is higher, sits on the road around the footprint. A lit yellow diamond sign with a ramp pictogram stands on a 6.6 m pole beyond the kerb on the ramp's side, 150 m before the toe. A landing kicks up dust in the road's colour, from `Look.dust` (sand at Salt Coast and Cinder Flats, cool grey in Lowtown), drawn as soft round sprites that grow from about 0.4 m to 1.5 m and fade out within half a second, sitting on the road rather than in it; a hard landing adds short bright spark streaks.

Bikes, riders, cars, and vans are glTF models in `apps/web/src/game/models`, loaded once by `assets.ts` before the first race. The scripts in `tools/blender` build them in two steps. First run `Blender -b -P tools/blender/bike.py`, and the same for `car.py`, `van.py`, and `rider_rig.py`; each writes an uncompressed GLB to `tools/blender/out`, which is not committed. Add `-- show` to render previews into the system's temporary folder. Then run `node tools/blender/compress.ts`, which writes the shipped models. It drops what the game never reads (texture coordinates, and every animation channel except the bones' rotations and the pelvis's translation), then applies meshopt compression with quantization: positions in 14 bits, normals in 8-bit octahedral form, skin weights in 8 bits, and pose rotations through meshopt's 16-bit quaternion filter. That took the six models from 4.6 MB to 0.94 MB. The worst error is about 0.3 mm on a vertex and 0.005° on a pose. Quantization places each static mesh through a node transform, and for the rider it folds that transform into the skin's inverse bind matrices. `assets.ts` bakes each static mesh's node transform into float geometry, so the merged meshes keep the space they had before. It leaves skinned geometry in bind space, where the inverse bind matrices already match it. Vite fingerprints the models into `/assets/`, and `apps/web/public/_headers` caches everything there as immutable for a year. A material's name is a colour slot. The loader merges each model into one vertex-coloured mesh and paints the slots a rider's or a car's colours, with lamps in a second mesh that glows. Riders are three builds of one skinned figure with 53 bones. A rider's 18 poses are frames of one animation, and `rider-figure.ts` blends between poses per bone, so a punch, a kick, a swing, a run, and a tumble are poses the simulation's state selects. The riding pose is built every frame from the simulation's numbers rather than picked: at load the figure bakes a tucked pose from the seated one by turning the spine, neck, head, and arms, and a hung pose for each side from both by rolling the spine 18° into the turn with the head counter-rolled 9°, plus a flinch pose for each side, and a risen pose for each side. The risen pose turns the spine upright and rolls the pelvis and spine away from the kicking side, about 26° in all, with the head counter-rolled to stay level; the figure is lifted 13 cm, shifted 7 cm toward the kicking side and 4 cm forward by moving the model under the rider's root, because the pelvis bone is held at the origin; and a two-bone solve on each leg and arm keeps the feet on the pegs and the hands on the grips at the lifted position. The kick pose for a side is the risen pose with the kicking leg's bones taken from the GLB's kick pose. `stance.rise` blends the riding pose toward the risen pose and moves the model by the same fraction. `rider.tuck` blends seated toward tucked, `rider.lean` over the bike's `visualLean` blends toward the hung pose on the leaning side, and `hitStun` blends toward the flinch, so a tucked rider in a bend rolls with the bike and hangs further into it, and a flick from one side to the other passes through the upright pose only as the lean itself passes through zero. Strike poses are blended from that riding pose by the swing's `reach`, so they never pop from a stale pose and never ease. Traffic is instanced: the car model for low vehicles and the van model, scaled, for vans and trucks.

The chase camera frames the race the way the original game did. It faces along the road's direction at the player's bike and never looks toward a point further up the road, so a bend swings the far road across the screen while the bike stays near the middle. It does not roll. Its field of view is 48 degrees at rest and widens to 57 degrees by 75 m/s, so speed stretches the road; a buffet of up to 3.5 cm shakes it between 38 and 78 m/s; and it settles 24 cm lower when the rider tucks in, following the tuck at 4 per second. There is no motion blur and there are no afterimages. It takes three quarters of the bike's sideways offset, and the bike crosses the screen for the last quarter. Its pitch, distance, and height are solved from the frame it must produce: the horizon 0.34 of the way down, the rear wheel 0.685 of the way down, and the rider 0.27 of the frame tall. Hits still jolt it. When the player is off the bike it follows whichever of the body and the bike is further back down the road, so the one that is further on is always in front of the camera and in frame: after a crash it stays with the bike and watches the body fly on, and a rider who runs past the bike takes the camera with them. The rider on foot faces the way they are moving and stands mid-stride when still; while rising from the ground they turn to face the bike. At the rear wheel a 1600 × 813 frame spans 13.9 m across, so on the roads with two lanes each way the paved road is wider than the frame (Slag Row 1.08, Breakwater Run 1.17, Cinder Wash 1.30 of the frame width) and on the one-lane roads it is not (Hollow Pine Road 0.58, Ridge Road 0.65).

React renders menus and the HUD only. During a race the HUD is one speedometer at the bottom centre: a ring of ticks that light with speed up to 300 km/h, the speed as a number, the gear as a small numeral inside the ring, the player's place out of the field, and an arc around the ring that fills with progress along the road. There are no stamina or condition bars; the rider and the bike show damage themselves. A rival's line of speech still floats over that rival, and the cop warning still appears. The game is loaded with a dynamic import after the page is interactive. The same effect starts the six model downloads first, through `models.ts`, which ships in the route's chunk rather than the game chunk, so the models and the game chunk arrive in parallel; `assets.ts` parses the bytes those downloads return rather than fetching again. `createGame` resolves once the models have loaded, and the game reports HUD values at 15 Hz. Besides speed, gear, place, and progress these are stamina, the bike's name and condition, the road's name and length, the distance to the nearest cop chasing the player at any range, and the distance left to the finish. The race screen decides when the cop's distance becomes a warning: under 80 m the badge turns red and blinks.

Until the career screens exist, the title screen and the default race rotate through the five roads at level 1. The attract race moves to the next road each time it ends, Ride starts on the road the title is showing, and every race after that takes the next road.

The target is 60 frames per second on an integrated-graphics laptop.

## Local development

`pnpm dev` runs `alchemy dev` with local state in `.alchemy/` and needs no Cloudflare account. The web app is at `http://localhost:1350`.

Alchemy is installed from its preview registry at `main` commit `e354a455`, because the latest npm beta does not support Effect 4.0.0. `pnpm-workspace.yaml` disables `blockExoticSubdeps` for its sibling tarballs. Move back to an npm release when one supports Effect 4.0.0.
