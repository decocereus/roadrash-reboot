# Browser client

`apps/web/src/game` builds the world from the compiled track: chunked road and terrain meshes, instanced scenery and traffic, a chase camera, and synthesized audio whose engine note follows the simulation's revs, from 52 Hz at idle to 300 Hz at the redline, and dips for the cut at every upshift. The loop steps the simulation at 60 Hz and draws interpolated poses at the display's refresh rate.

The game holds a constant frame rate. Before the first race, `presets.ts` reads the WebGL renderer string once and picks one preset from a table, and nothing changes that preset afterwards: there is no governor and no quality change during a race, whatever the frame times do. A preset is a shadow map size, a bloom scale, a multisampling count and a pixel budget. `full` is the 4096 shadow map, four samples and the screen's native pixel ratio, up to 2. `balanced` and `lean` use a 2048 shadow map and no multisampling, and hold the canvas to 2560 x 1440 and 1344 x 756 worth of pixels, which is 0.7 of a 1080p screen. `lean` also draws glow at half size and gives rivals under 80 pixels tall a 4,000-triangle body. Every preset takes nine shadow samples per pixel in place of three's seventeen, which `cel.ts` patches into the shadow shader when it loads. The check picks cautiously: Apple's Pro, Max and Ultra chips and GeForce GTX, RTX and Radeon RX or Pro cards get `full`, any other Apple GPU gets `balanced`, including Safari, which reports every Mac as `Apple GPU`, and everything else gets `lean`, including Intel, an unknown name or a software renderer. The pixel budget is a count of pixels rather than a scale, so a Retina laptop and a 1080p laptop draw the same number on the same preset, and resizing the window keeps it. The preset goes to the pipeline and the world when they are built, so no render target is reallocated for quality. If a chosen frame rate cannot be held on a device, the fix is a cheaper preset for that device or a cheaper scene, not a change mid-race. On a 60 Hz or 120 Hz screen the game draws 60 frames a second; Settings, Frame rate, also offers 30. The pacer in `pacer.ts` skips callbacks to hold that rate on a faster screen. Once a second the refresh meter checks the rate it holds: the vsyncs the pacer counted at that rate over the last second, a gap of three vsyncs counting three, must add up to the time that passed, within 3%. A GPU that misses vsyncs leaves gaps of whole vsyncs, which still add up, so a slow GPU never moves the rate and the game draws no extra frames when it catches up. A rate that is a whole multiple of the screen's, such as 120 on a 60 Hz screen, adds up too and paces the same frames. When the count stops adding up, the meter reads the screen's period from the shortest gaps, because a missed vsync only makes a gap whole periods longer: it drops the shortest tenth as strays, takes the shortest gap left, and averages the gaps that span one of it. It follows that rate when the gaps add up at it too and two readings in a row agree, so moving the window from a 165 Hz screen to a 144 Hz one takes the game from 48 frames a second to 72 within 3 s. A device's pixel ratio is read each callback, so dragging the window to another screen resizes the canvas on the next frame, before it draws. `?preset=` pins a preset, `?ratio=` pins the pixel ratio and turns the pacer off to measure uncapped frame cost, and `?hz=` pins the detected refresh. The frame meter, turned on in Settings or with `?fps`, shows frame rate, the raw frame time and worst frame (not the 0.1 s clamped value the simulation steps by), the simulation time that clamp threw away, the pixel ratio, the preset, the pace, the detected refresh, draw calls and triangles. Until November 2026 a governor stepped through a ladder of eight quality rungs mid-race, and Auto was the default setting; the owner replaced both with fixed presets, because every step resized every render target and read as a nosedive in resolution, and on the UHD 630 Auto ran at about 21.6 fps on the same rung that a setting of 60 ran at about 39.

Play tests on other machines are recorded, when a tester opens the game with `?tester` and turns recording on, by `apps/web/src/diagnostics`. The hub there is created before the game loads and owns the session (a UUID per page load, the build's commit and dirty flag stamped by `vite.config.ts`, and the device the tester picked), the device-local settings under `rushline.settings.v1`, the recorder, and the uploader. In the frame loop the recorder only writes numbers into preallocated rings; JSON and network work happen in a 30 second batch outside it, and a period with no frames and no events sends nothing, so a tab left on a menu or in the background stops filling the store with empty batches. The game numbers the canvas's drawing buffer: every resize is a new generation, each frame notes the generation it drew, and at the start of the next frame a mismatch is recorded as a frame a resize cleared before it was presented, which is how the black flash fixed on October 16, 2026 showed up. `packages/telemetry` holds the wire contract: `limits.ts` with no dependencies for the browser, and `schema.ts`, the Effect schema `POST /api/telemetry` (`apps/web/src/routes/api/telemetry.ts`, `apps/web/src/server/telemetry.ts`) validates against before an injected store acknowledges the batch. `vite.config.ts` injects a JSONL store for `vite dev` and `vite preview`, so `node:fs` never reaches the Worker; a store for the deployed Worker is not built yet. `tools/perf/README.md` has the play-server command and the session CLI.

`?stress` starts a fixed worst-case race the moment the game loads and puts the player on the pilot, in the production build too: Slag Row with tier 3's field of fifteen, seed 8, eight police, rivals at their most aggressive toward the player, and pacing that holds the pack around the player. `?stress=reference` is the ordinary Slag Row race with tier 1's field on seed 31. `packages/career/src/bench.ts` builds both setups from public setup functions and changes no rule, `apps/web/src/career/bench.ts` reads the URL, and the measured windows are `benchWindows` in `packages/sim/src/bench-window.ts`: ticks 2028 to 2628 of the stress race and 3849 to 4449 of the reference race. In the stress window 12 or 13 rivals ride within 150 m ahead of the player on every tick. `?prof`, which `?stress` implies, records each frame's CPU time per part (sim, riders, fleet, camera, world, matrices, the shadow map, and each render pass) and the draw calls and triangles per pass into preallocated arrays, read through `globalThis.__rushlineProfile`; `?prof=gpu` adds timer queries per pass, which ANGLE on Metal answers with numbers too large to trust. `tools/perf/measure.ts` drives headless Chrome through it. The perf gate holds the game to `tools/perf/budgets.json`: `pnpm perf` checks simulation time, allocations and bytes, locally and in CI, and `pnpm perf:gpu` checks frame pacing and render counts on this Mac's GPU only. `tools/perf/README.md` has the commands.

Starting a race does not compile shaders. The ride-out card asks the game to `prepare` its plan: the game builds the road, the field, and the traffic while the card is up, compiles the new materials with `compileAsync`, which uses `KHR_parallel_shader_compile` where the browser has it, and then draws the start grid once behind the screen. Start stays inactive, labelled as loading the road, until that finishes, because starting on half-compiled shaders would block the first frame until they were done. Start then only begins the countdown. Every load builds the new world and actors before it disposes the old ones, so a program the two share is never dropped and compiled again, and the title's attract race compiles in the background while the loop holds its last frame. The pipeline compiles with three's synchronous `compile`, which hands the programs to the driver, and then polls `KHR_parallel_shader_compile` itself over `renderer.info.programs` rather than using `compileAsync`. three's poller reads the properties of every material it compiled and cannot be stopped, so disposing one of them, or losing the context, made its next poll throw, and a poll that never finished held whatever waited on it. The game's poller only reads the renderer's live program list, ignores a program three has already destroyed, and stops when asked, so old worlds and actors are disposed as soon as the new ones are built and teardown is immediate. A newer load stops the older poll. `preparation.ts` ends each preparation with one outcome: `ready`; `timedOut` when the compile is still pending after five seconds; `failed`, with the stage (`build`, `compile` or `warm`) and the error thrown; `lost` when the WebGL context is lost; or `cancelled` when a newer load or the unmount replaced it. After a timeout or a failed compile the race still warms up the old synchronous way, a stall rather than a Start button that never comes back, and a failure is recorded as a recorder fault from `prepare <stage>`. The pre-race screen enables Start once preparation ends with anything but `cancelled`. While the context is lost nothing compiles or warms, and Start only begins the countdown. The race, its world and its actors stay in memory; three re-creates its GPU state when the browser restores the context, and the next preparation or frame uploads the scene again, so a lost context never resets a race. A replaced world belongs to the build that replaced it and is disposed exactly once, by that build's release.

The handoff from preparing a race to riding it is two transition tables: `apps/web/src/race-handoff.ts` for the ride-out card and `apps/web/src/room-handoff.ts` for a room's seat, which stays out of the first load with the lobby. Each takes an event, such as the game being ready, a prepare outcome or the dwell ending, and returns the next state and at most one effect. `useHandoff` in `components/use-handoff.ts` runs the effect, which calls `prepare`, reports the seat loaded, leaves the seat or starts the race. `preparedRace` turns a prepare outcome into the event both tables read. The card starts once its race is built and its dwell is over, and a race that was not built offers Retry and Back. A seat reports loaded once its race is built and the door has had its 5 seconds, then starts when the room says racing, and a race that was not built leaves the seat and moves back to the room. The two tables stay apart because only the card has a retry and only the seat has a room to wait for. A table with a setting for each difference would hold rows that one path can never reach.

Which screen the app shows is one more transition function, `stepNavigation` in `apps/web/src/navigation.ts`, which has no React import. `View` is the screen union. A race's `stage` is `riding`, `paused`, `finishing` with its report, or `consequence` with the report to show when the game says the race is over. Every screen has one way out, the `left` event, and `leaving` decides where it goes. `useFlow` turns the rules and the save into the `entered` event and the game's callbacks into race events, and runs the effects: `beginCareer`, `decodeBar`, `openBar` and `rematch`. `apps/web/test/navigation.test.ts` walks whole sessions through it with no browser. It reads the race session, the handoffs and the save through those events and keeps no copy of them.

A game owns everything it makes through one list of owners in `createGame`: the parsed models, the renderer, the pipeline, the world, the sound, the actors, the input, the listeners and observers, and the animation loop. Dispose releases them once, in reverse order, and so does a construction that throws partway; a second dispose does nothing. A world owns its scene, its textures, its shadow map and the cel ramp in its `Shading`; actors and figures use the world's ramp and never release it. `disposeTree` releases each geometry and material once however many meshes or point clouds share it, and leaves textures to whoever made them. Warm-up (`staging.ts`) reveals hidden meshes, arms every weapon, primes the sparks and adds sample meshes so that each program compiles before the race, and puts all of it back even when the draw or the setup throws. Before October 16, 2026 the Start click did all of this at once: about 200 ms on the M4 Pro and 300 ms on the Air.

The race session is one value, `Session` in `apps/web/src/game/game-session.ts`, and its `phase` is what the HUD reads. A variant holds only what is true in it: a race in its countdown or on the road has its plan and log, a settled race has its outcome, and a preparation exists only while idle. The named transitions there are pure, so `apps/web/test/game-session.test.ts` drives them with no WebGL, and `game.ts` performs the effects around them. The countdown, the resume hold and the consequence delay change in place, because the tick path allocates nothing.

Nothing is drawn that the fog has already hidden. The road, kerb, walk, terrain, and bank chunks are 200 m long and only the chunks within `fogFar` plus 200 m ahead of the player and 260 m behind are visible, the same reach as the city's chunks; the camera's far plane stays at 6 km for the sky, the plain, and the mountains, which do not take fog. Traffic is one instanced mesh per model part, and each frame only the cars within that reach ahead and 40 m behind are written into it, so a road's hundred-odd cars cost the vertex shader only when they can be seen. On Slag Row, the heaviest road, that took a frame at ratio 2 from about 900 draw calls and 5.7 million triangles to about 600 and 2.9 million. The frame there is bound by the CPU side of three's draw submission, not the GPU, so draw calls are the number to watch. People are shorter-sighted still: `walkers.ts` writes only the walkers and market crowd within 300 m ahead and 40 m behind, because each costs about 5,400 triangles in each of three passes and is about 5 pixels tall at that distance.

Nothing the camera passes covers the view. The chase camera follows three quarters of the rider's swing across the road, so a rider on the shoulder carries it to the verge. `clearance.ts` hides a roadside prop, all its parts together, while the camera is within 2 m of any of them, and shows it again once the camera has left. A wider band reaches the pine crowns beside the narrow forest roads from the road itself, and hides them while they are on screen. Each part counts at its real footprint, so a pine counts at the width of its crown and a sign at the width of its board. `plant` registers every instanced placement it plants, and gantry posts and finish gates join as objects, so a new prop joins by being planted. The clearance keys props by 8 m cells and checks only the camera's cell and the props it has hidden, and hides an instance by writing a zero matrix into a one-item upload span. It hides a grouped prop's meshes, not the chunk above them, so it never brings back a chunk the fog reach has culled. Banks and building fronts stand beyond the band. City awnings and hanging signs come within 2 m of the eye only beside or behind it, with the rider thrown against the building line, so they stay batched in their chunks and do not take part. A market is one merged mesh per material, so `addSpans` registers each stall and decoration as ranges of the merged index buffer, and hiding one writes zeros over its range and uploads only that range.

`bazaar.ts` draws a road's `Track.stalls` from `models/stalls.glb`. It bakes every stall and decoration into one cel mesh and one glow mesh for the whole market, costing two draw calls in the main pass, two in the normal pass and none in the shadow pass. It chooses the decorations from the road's seed, and the simulation does not know they exist. `bazaar-crowd.ts` stands people at every third stall, from the same list, serving, haggling and browsing. They are drawn on the walker instances with the `serve`, `haggle` and `browse` clips, and they are not race state, so nothing can hit them and a room never hears of them.

Every surface is drawn in two tones. `cel.ts` makes the materials: a toon ramp with one hard step, a hard rim strip on the side facing the sky's light, and a hard highlight on glossy paint. The shadow tone is the ambient light and the lit tone is ambient plus the one directional light, so a look states both tones as the colours they should reach on screen. Riders and bikes are the exception: `shading.figure` lights their lit band with the figure light at the look's brightness and draws its shadow band as a darker, slightly turned shade of the lit colour worked out in OKLab, so a rider keeps the colours of its sheet, and a bike its paint, in light and shadow under every look. Each figure also tests the sun's shadow map at a point above it, so its rim, shine and shadow depth follow the sun and shade it rides through ([art-direction.md](../design/art-direction.md#budget-and-build) has the recipe). Traffic and the world keep the look's tones. Riders, bikes and traffic share the painted cel shader source, and a per-material uniform chooses the rule, so the rule adds no program variant. `post.ts` draws the frame through a fixed chain: the scene, ink outlines found from a normal and depth pass, bloom on anything brighter than a lit wall, a colour fringe and vignette, then output. Sky, clouds, cables, and far ground sit on a second layer the outline pass does not see. One 4096 shadow map follows the camera. The normal and depth pass draws each mesh with the side its own material draws, so the back of a double-sided sign hides what stands behind it from the outlines as well.

`looks.ts` holds a look per region: sky, fog, the two tones, light directions, outline colour, wall and road colours, window and lamp colours, bloom, and whether the road is wet. A look also picks a kit. Salt Coast is a city at dusk and Lowtown is a wet city at night. Stonepeak is an open ridge at dusk. The city kit in `city.ts` lines both sides of the road with buildings generated from the track's seed: shopfronts, towers, awnings, balconies, blade signs, billboards, and cables. It builds them in 200 m chunks around the player. Building fronts stand at the distance where the simulation crashes a rider who leaves the road, so what looks solid is solid. The open kit keeps the terrain, trees, rocks, and a ring of mountains, and `roadside.ts` dresses the edge of the road from the track's seed: cut banks of faceted rock, a marker post with a red reflector every 28 m, boulders, and warning signs. Each look says in its `roadside` how much of each it gets. Banks, posts, trees, and boulders stand at the same distance as the city's building fronts, just past the verge where the simulation throws a rider who leaves the road, so nothing the renderer adds can be ridden into without the simulation knowing. The banks are merged per 200 m chunk; everything else is instanced. `ground.ts` places things on the ground: the terrain lift beside the road, the kerb, and the instancing of scattered props. Every texture is drawn on a canvas at load, through the helpers in `texture-canvas.ts`: the facades in `facade-texture.ts`, the shopfronts in `shop-texture.ts`, the sign atlas in `sign-atlas.ts`, the road with its lines and the paving in `road-texture.ts`, the sky in `sky-texture.ts`, and the chevrons, the warning sign and the start and finish banners in `sign-textures.ts`. The road texture follows the lane count: solid edge lines at the outer lane edges, the double centre line, a dashed divider between the two same-way lanes where a road has two lanes each way, and a band of wheel wear down every lane centre. A tile is 24 m long and 512 texels across on a one-lane road, 1024 on a two-lane road, so a line is about the same number of texels wide on both.

`hazards.ts` draws `RaceState.hazards` every frame, not the track's schedule, so a hazard spawned mid-race appears at once. Each kind has one instanced mesh per part, sized from `hazardSizes`: oil is a glossy black slick: a dark lobed pool, a faint ring of dark teal, plum, and olive sheen, and a pale band of reflected sky across its far end, all lit like the road and nothing that glows; a pothole is a near-black hole whose far inner wall catches the light, ringed by a broken asphalt edge lighter than the road and a low raised lip 5 cm high, so its edge shows even when the hole is seen nearly edge-on; a rock is a small faceted stone the size of its footprint in the region's rock colour with a soft contact shadow, on every road. Hazards are drawn at their real size and are not made to stand out: seeing one late is part of the game. Only hazards from 40 m behind the camera's rider to the fog plus 200 m ahead are written into the instances, and the buffers are rewritten only when that set or a hazard's position changes. A `jolt` or a `bump` kicks the bike's suspension sag, and a `stagger`, a `bump`, or a hazard crash near the camera throws up road dust. The DEV ride flags `?hazard=<id>&before=<m>&kmh=<speed>` start the player that far before a hazard, on its line, at that speed; `&straight` holds full throttle and steers to keep the bike on the hazard's line through bends; `&clear` moves traffic out of the way from the start to 400 m past the hazard; and `&hold=near&holdafter=<m>` freezes the frame when the player is that close to it, alongside `hold=hazard` for the first touch.

The truck and its six deck cars are part of the traffic fleet in `fleet.ts`. A landing kicks up dust in the road's colour, from `Look.dust` (sand at Salt Coast, cool grey in Lowtown), drawn as soft round sprites that grow from about 0.4 m to 1.5 m and fade out within half a second, sitting on the road rather than in it; a hard landing adds short bright spark streaks.

Bikes, riders, cars, and vans are glTF models in the art store (see [Game art](game-art.md)), named `models/<name>.glb` in `apps/web/src/assets/art.json`. The bikes, the generic riders, the car and the van are loaded once by `assets.ts` before the first race; a celebrity rider is loaded when a race dresses a rider in it, because a celebrity is in its field or a dev flag names it. The scripts in `tools/blender` build them in two steps. First run `Blender -b -P tools/blender/bike.py`, and the same for `car.py`, `van.py`, and `rider_rig.py`; each writes an uncompressed GLB to `tools/blender/out`, which is not committed. Add `-- show` to render previews into the system's temporary folder. Then run `node tools/blender/compress.ts`, which stages the shipped models into the art store; run `pnpm art:upload` before you push. It drops what the game never reads (texture coordinates on parts with no base-colour texture, every texture except the base colour, and every animation channel except the bones' rotations and the pelvis's translation), writes each base-colour texture as lossless WebP at most 1024 px on a side, then applies meshopt compression with quantization: positions in 14 bits, normals in 8-bit octahedral form, skin weights in 8 bits, and pose rotations through meshopt's 16-bit quaternion filter. That took the six models from 4.6 MB to 0.94 MB. The worst error is about 0.3 mm on a vertex and 0.005° on a pose. Quantization places each static mesh through a node transform, and for the rider it folds that transform into the skin's inverse bind matrices. `assets.ts` bakes each static mesh's node transform into float geometry, so the merged meshes keep the space they had before. It leaves the first skinned mesh's geometry in bind space, where its inverse bind matrices already match it. Quantization gives each skinned mesh its own transform, so a rider whose helmet is a second skinned mesh has two sets of inverse bind matrices; `assets.ts` moves every later skinned mesh into the first one's bind space before merging, and the figure binds to the first skin. Each model is served at `/art/<key>`, where the key is its content hash, as immutable for a year. A material's name is a colour slot, and `model-slots.ts` gives each slot its policy: painted from the palette the game hands it, a fixed colour, a lamp, or the colours it was modelled with. The loader refuses a model with a material no policy names, and painting refuses a painted slot its palette has no colour for, so no part is drawn in a colour nobody chose. The loader merges each model into one vertex-coloured mesh and paints the slots a rider's or a car's colours, with lamps in a second mesh that glows. A traffic car's paint indexes `trafficPaints` in `packages/sim/src/palette.ts`, the same list the sim's `PAINT_COUNT` counts. Generic riders are three builds of one skinned figure with 53 bones. The six painted celebrities, `rider_<id>.glb` for each id in `celebrityIds` in `packages/sim/src/cast.ts` (`kylie`, `ronaldo`, `mbappe`, `musk`, `altman` and `ranveer`), share that skeleton and its poses but colour every part from one sRGB albedo atlas instead of slots. Their vertices are white, the painted cel material samples the atlas through three's own map path, and the palette never tints them, so a celebrity is still one skinned mesh and one draw. The loader refuses a rider that mixes atlas parts and colour slots and keeps the atlas with the parsed rider. No celebrity loads at boot. `prepare` works out which of the race's riders wear which celebrity (`wornBy`), and the game's wardrobe (`figure-wardrobe.ts`) loads each one it names once, through the asset runtime, before it builds the race, so a plain race downloads and decodes none. The race is built from the wardrobe's dressing, a map from `RiderId` to the loaded rider. `Assets` holds no celebrity, so no figure can be built from one that has not loaded. The wardrobe holds a celebrity while the race on screen wears it, so the next race with the same rider fetches and parses nothing, and a restart reuses the dressing. Once a race is built without it, or the game is disposed, the wardrobe releases it: the atlas is disposed and its decoded bitmap closed. A later race that wears it again parses it from the page's byte cache without fetching. Starting a race that was never prepared, as `?ride=` and `?stress` do at boot, prepares it first. The cast does not name the celebrities yet. In a dev build `?as=<id>` dresses the player as one and `?against=<id>,<id>` dresses the first rival racers in order; the attract race wears none. A rider's 18 poses are frames of one animation, and `rider-figure.ts` blends between poses per bone, so a punch, a kick, a swing, a run, and a tumble are poses the simulation's state selects. The riding pose is built every frame from the simulation's numbers rather than picked: at load the figure bakes, in `figure-pose.ts`, a tucked pose from the seated one by turning the spine, neck, head, and arms, and a hung pose for each side from both by rolling the spine 18° into the turn with the head counter-rolled 9°, plus a flinch pose for each side, and a risen pose for each side. The risen pose turns the spine upright and rolls the pelvis and spine away from the kicking side, about 26° in all, with the head counter-rolled to stay level; the figure is lifted 13 cm, shifted 7 cm toward the kicking side and 4 cm forward by moving the model under the rider's root, because the pelvis bone is held at the origin. The kick pose for a side is the risen pose with the kicking leg's bones taken from the GLB's kick pose. `stance.rise` blends the riding pose toward the risen pose and moves the model by the same fraction. `rider.tuck` blends seated toward tucked, `rider.lean` over the bike's `visualLean` blends toward the hung pose on the leaning side, and `hitStun` blends toward the flinch, so a tucked rider in a bend rolls with the bike and hangs further into it, and a flick from one side to the other passes through the upright pose only as the lean itself passes through zero. Strike poses are blended from that riding pose by the swing's `reach`, so they never pop from a stale pose and never ease. Traffic is instanced: the car model for low vehicles and the van model, scaled, for vans and trucks. Each maker's bikes share one model, chosen by `bikeModelOf` in `apps/web/src/game/bike-models.ts`. Ostra rides the Seraph, Halden the Ghost, Brack the Oni, and the Gutter Pup the original bike. The three cyber models come from `tools/blender/cyber_bikes.py` (see `docs/art/cyber-bike-options/README.md`) and keep the original's part names and colour slots, so one rider rig fits all four. `actors.ts` keeps one instanced fleet per model in the race, and turns each wheel about the centre of its tyre, with the radius read from the tyre's height when the fleet is built, so a wheel rolls one turn per circumference on every model.

A rider's riding poses start from the seated pose, which holds the hands on the grips and the feet on the pegs. `figure-pose.ts` turns the spine, neck and arms for the tuck, the lean, the flinch and the rise, and the figure blends those poses every frame by the race's tuck, lean, rise and flinch. Turning the body moves the shoulders and hips, so after each frame's blend `planterOf` puts every planted hand of the pose on screen back on its grip and every planted foot back on its peg, at the figure's current lift. It swings the clavicle up to 18 degrees toward a grip the arm cannot reach, the widest clavicle turn the authored poses use. If that is not enough it pitches the torso forward, up to the tuck's 29 degrees. Then it bends the elbow or knee in the plane it already bends in and holds the hand or foot at its seated angle. A punch, windup or swing leaves the striking arm free, and a kick leaves the kicking leg free; a strike blends out of the planted riding pose, so the free limb leaves its grip or peg and returns to it. The rise is what needs the clavicle and the torso: generic build B's arm is straight in the seated pose, so it pitches 21 degrees forward to stand up for a kick, and the other riders pitch 1 to 14 degrees. Build B also swings a clavicle and pitches 1 degree in a full lean. `apps/web/test/planted-hands.test.ts` holds every shipped rider's planted palms within 5 mm of the grips in every riding pose, through a tucked corner and as a kick drops back to the seat.

The chase camera frames the race the way the original game did. It faces along the road's direction at the player's bike and never looks toward a point further up the road, so a bend swings the far road across the screen while the bike stays near the middle. It does not roll. Its field of view is 48 degrees at rest and widens to 57 degrees by 75 m/s, so speed stretches the road; a buffet of up to 3.5 cm shakes it between 38 and 78 m/s; and it settles 24 cm lower when the rider tucks in, following the tuck at 4 per second. There is no motion blur and there are no afterimages. It takes three quarters of the bike's sideways offset, and the bike crosses the screen for the last quarter. Its pitch, distance, and height are solved from the frame it must produce: the horizon 0.34 of the way down, the rear wheel 0.685 of the way down, and the rider 0.27 of the frame tall. Hits still jolt it. When the player is off the bike it follows whichever of the body and the bike is further back down the road, so the one that is further on is always in front of the camera and in frame: after a crash it stays with the bike and watches the body fly on, and a rider who runs past the bike takes the camera with them. The rider on foot faces the way they are moving and stands mid-stride when still; while rising from the ground they turn to face the bike. At the rear wheel a 1600 × 813 frame spans 13.9 m across, so on the roads with two lanes each way the paved road is wider than the frame (Slag Row 1.08, Breakwater Run 1.17 of the frame width) and on the one-lane road it is not.

React renders menus and the HUD only. During a race the HUD is one speedometer at the bottom centre: a ring of ticks that light with speed up to 300 km/h, the speed as a number, the gear as a small numeral inside the ring, the player's place out of the field, and an arc around the ring that fills with progress along the road. There are no stamina or condition bars; the rider and the bike show damage themselves. The cop warning still appears. Nothing names a rival or speaks during a race. The game is loaded with a dynamic import after the page is interactive. `apps/web/src/asset-load.ts` starts two dynamic imports together, the small asset runtime, which starts the twelve boot model downloads as soon as it arrives, and the game chunk, so the models and the game chunk arrive in parallel. Each model is parsed by `assets.ts` once the game chunk is in, and the parsed models belong to that mount: unmounting aborts the downloads and releases whatever was parsed, and a later mount parses the cached bytes again ([errors.md](../errors.md) has the failure and retry rules). `createGame` receives the loaded models and builds the game at once, and the game publishes HUD values at 15 Hz of processed frame time. `hud-cadence.ts` adds each rendered frame's seconds, clamped at 0.1 s, and when the total reaches 1/15 s it publishes once and starts again from zero. A 60 Hz display therefore publishes on every fourth frame, 30 Hz on every second and 120 Hz on every eighth; a long frame publishes once and drops the rest, and nothing builds up while the loop is halted. Preparing a race, the attract loop, quitting, sleeping and muting publish at once, outside that clock. So does a room stall starting or ending: the frame loop passes whether the race is stopped on a silent room to `advanceHud`, which publishes on the frame that changes and starts its clock again. Besides speed, gear, place, and progress these are stamina, the bike's name and condition, the road's name and length, the distance to the nearest cop chasing the player at any range, and the distance left to the finish. The race screen decides when the cop's distance becomes a warning: under 80 m the badge turns red and blinks.

The attract race loads at boot behind the loading screen and rotates through the three roads, moving to the next road each time it ends; nobody sees it since the title screen was cut on October 23, 2026.

The target is 60 frames per second on an integrated-graphics laptop.

## The bar lobby

The bar is the room players walk around before a race, and it is the lobby of every race room, public or custom. `/bar` opens it with no room in a development build. `packages/sim/src/lobby-floor.ts` holds the room's layout as data, so the props the browser draws and the solids a rider collides with read the same numbers, and `packages/sim/src/lobby.ts` holds the movement rules at a fixed 60 Hz tick. A rider starts on foot and is `foot`, on a `stool` or on the `bike`. A click plans a route across the floor once, a breadth-first search over a 25 cm grid straightened by line of sight, and a key press drops it. A shove pushes the other rider away and fades in about half a second. The bike is hidden: the rider walks behind the bar through the gap past the counter's left end and presses `E` at the taps, and `B` then hops on or off anywhere for the rest of the page's life.

`apps/web/src/game/bar.ts` builds the scene and steps nothing itself. `bar-player.ts` turns input into those rules and interpolates between ticks, `bar-walker.ts` poses a figure from the result, `bar-camera.ts` owns the Angled, Follow and Whole room shots, and `bar-cast.ts` holds seven stand-in riders. The player's camera choice is saved in the browser by `apps/web/src/bar-view.ts`.

`/bar?room=<id>&name=<name>` puts the same room on a race room's seat. `bar-members.ts` gives each member of the room a figure in the outfit of their place in the server's member list, so every browser dresses a member alike, sends this player's walk, and follows the others' walks with smoothing. `lobby-walk.ts` holds the walk's shape apart from the floor grid, so the Worker can read it without loading the grid. Each browser simulates only its own rider. A shove is a message naming its target, and the target's browser moves its own rider, so nobody's position has two authors. In a room the stand-ins stay off the floor and only the display riders on the bikes remain.

`components/room-lobby.tsx` renders the bar for every lobby on a seat. The status screens in `screens/room-lobby.tsx` cover the rest: joining, the invite, a refused seat, and the wait for assets when a race starts with the seat open and no lobby yet. `components/bar-lobby.tsx` is the panel over the canvas. It shows the members, who is ready, the rules and the ready button, and `Enter` presses it. A custom room's host picks the rules behind the rules button, and a public room has no host, no rule picker and no link to copy. When the server starts the race, the panel goes, the door lifts, the other members walk to the door on riders each browser steps for itself, and walks stop being sent, because the host relays nothing to a seated rider. Each browser holds its `loaded` message until the door has had 5 seconds, so a fast machine still sees the door, and the existing countdown then cuts to the race. That hold means every race's `readyMs` and `waitedMs` read at least 5 s. The game sleeps on every screen but the race, so no race draws under the bar. The game, the renderer and the same 12 models load in the background whenever `/` opens, including its menus and room links, so the bar's download cost is its own code, about 25 KB gzip, and the bar chunk loads only when a player enters a room. The bar picks its preset with `presetFor` on the graphics card, as a race does, and `?lean` forces the `lean` preset.
